Otogi2:Immortal Warriors (***1/2)


a review of
Otogi 2:Immortal Warriors
a videogame developed by From Software
and published by sega
text by Samuel Kite
score: 1/2 (out of four)

Bottom line: Otogi 2:Immortal Warriors is “is a fighting game in which an entire level is your opponent”.


1/2


The secret to peeing in public is to concentrate really intently on what you’re doing, as though driving recklessly. I say driving recklessly, because without the thrill of the possibility that failure could lead to organ inversion, there’s no way something as fundamentally capable of bored autopilot could possibly be so amazing.

Otogi: Mirth Mast of Demons (mist marked?) was a game that reveled in its bizarrely perfect presentation of Japanese anime quirks, yet suffered under every single burden with which the Meiji era has saddled the otherwise pure people of Japan, like oxen saddled with a yoke, in the sense that they are yoked rather than saddled. What I’m saying is that, from the point of view of the ox, they’re saddled with this cart pulling job that they didn’t ask for, whereas we normally think of the term yoke as being a management level abuse; this thing will be yoked to achieve some alteration to the bottom line (black!), whereas saddled is the plight of the worker. Who is this asshole, and why am I carrying them across a river?

While Tom Cruise has committed many atrocities, the volleyball scene in Top Gun is not one of them, and neither is The Last Samurai. This movie retreads the tired premise that Western life is sick in some essential sense, or is, at least, if you can’t swing with it an essenceless existence, and, if a kind of purity supposedly present in pastoral Japanese culture (as supposedly personified in the anything-but-pastoral, highly technological warrior priesthood of the samurai — emphasis on warrior, de-emphasis on priest, though I suppose, pound for pound, the innocent victims of abusive samurai are roughly equal to the innocent victims of priests as an abstract bucket in a high level planning meeting by Lucifer re: creation, and how to defile it) turns your crank, then that’s what you should go be, and to hell with the impracticalities layered like a bean dip for the orange bowl. The Japanese, being (mind-bogglingly) Japanese, are nothing but a giant crank turned by their own pastoral roots in the Shintoistic (jingoistic) conception of a spiritual life unified in its comprehensive ambiguity. Ancient japan. Where the Oni are Oni, the Kami are Kami, and the sheep are scared. Or imported and then scared.

Sorry, I mean a public restroom. Not literal public urination.

The Japanese are psychotic. They accidentally fitted several overlapping European cultural standards to their society without thinking the process through in order to catch up to the modern world as a nation befitting the degree of respect and power which, as the center of a known universe that extended a mere fifty miles offshore (seemingly implying the hookers in japan were far advanced in comparison to the rest of the world even in ancient times, making the return to port worth it, even in the face of pliable native women and fresh fruit or bizarre monsters such as kiwi birds to screw) they felt they needed to believe themselves to be accorded (entitled), whether they felt they actually desired to believe they should be accorded it (r-e-s-p-e-c-t). Oddly, by harnessing their gracious tolerance of capricious environs inhabited by spirits who kept it real like 24/7 by A) not giving a fuck, yo, and B) straight up living for the Here and Now, holmes (Katie), they might have met the introduction of Admiral Perry’s black ships with a “meh . . . what are you going to do desu ka,” which, in Japanese I think translates as, “nandemo kandemo na,” made their first train thirty years ago, deprived us all of reliable Toyota sports bikes, and been much, much, much happier, all told. However, they were in the grips (thrall) of their own catholic authoritarian institution at the time, the Emperor and accompanying court, which thought the mental well-being for the next three-hundred years of their society was an acceptable sacrifice in the name of being able to posture on the world stage, with the various European powers. This bullshit belief, and the seemingly fetishistic kowtowing of the Japanese people to authority, is what eventually necessitated a bright white light, and the beckoning of dead relatives (and then, again, several days later). The Japanese didn’t, and don’t, enjoy going through it any more than we enjoy uncomfortably justifying our choices in a strident and awkward way, and the reason they’re in a self-torturing cycle is because of bean-dip on top of their original vision. Whenever something goes wrong in Japanese art, design, or industry, it comes from the legacy of wrote memorization of Western tropes from the 1800s.

Otogi.

A deadly samurai spirit, with no particular self awareness, and a very, very limited time in the world in which to dart and fly, like a butterfly made out of razorblades, is on a mission to . . . oh, hey, was I just able to destroy that entire three-story temple? Holy shit! What else can I destroy? Oh, sweet! I can blow up these trees and I can . . . *game ends*.

What was that about? Well. If you’re German, you know that if the train is scheduled to arrive at the station at 4:37, then that is the moment it will arrive, and if it is 4:38 when the train arrives, perhaps we just shouldn’t bother having fucking trains, since obviously some of you are incapable of doing even the SIMPLEST FUCKING TASK (I’m talking to you, Hans *backhand* — now get out of my sight. We’ll have to do the ENTIRE thing with robots!). The Japanese did not have trains, and if they did, it wouldn’t have mattered to them if they arrived a few minutes late, or early, so long as the person running that train could take pride in his work, and make it run as precisely and as efficiently as he could — and, more specifically, that trains ran so often that it didn’t matter.

You see, the Japanese, stuck as they were either having to climb 100,000 stairs, or staying densely packed together, learned to master the fraternal art of “Hey, man, whatever makes you happy.” They may not have genuine respect for each other, which is universal and freely given — they’re not superhuman, for chrissake, but they had a remarkably gentle adaptation to the tolerance of personal foibles. There wasn’t anything wrong with being gay in japan, before Christians came and ruined everyone’s good time. There wasn’t anything wrong with being a pedophile, either (a tradition still proudly practiced today!). What was wrong was acting on these urges in such a way that everyone became silently uncomfortable. By making the awkward pause the greatest possible public punishment (aside from dismemberment by passing Ronin, or pressure-point activated kidney detonations from bored ninja), the Japanese had a remarkable capacity to embrace one another as kith and kin. Europeans do not do this. We shake the tree until fruit falls out, and we only pick up what looks good. That’s great if you’re ripe, or might turn ripe in the bowl a couple days later, but if you have a worm, it sucks. It also doesn’t work, as in the case of the Japanese, if you’re shaking a flowering plum tree, and all you do is divest it of its blossoms too quickly, and damage the circulatory system so that it doesn’t fruit except much later in the season, and sure, while they’re *more* fruit, since the tree took so long, they are smaller and more bitter. I guess you could argue that makes decent Umeboshi, but let’s not pretend that’s better than fresh, sweet plums in season.

You will also most likely disrupt a picnic.

Otogi’s picnic of graceful straight ahead destruction in beautiful environments against bizarre, seemingly arbitrary enemies, is ruined by the desire to make it some kind of Gran Turismo version of Ghostbusters, where the limited resource of health and magic (which is health, only with a caching system designed to confuse you just enough to be irritating) form the stopwatch which determines if you have managed to rank S, or merely A, while clawing your way around the track in a 40cc ultra-minivan in the “almost a motorcycle” vehicle class. If you intend to succeed, then you must learn your car, and the track, and get through it; Western pig. As if you could, with all your disgusting flab and awkwardly bumpy bodies. No amount of slaughtering bears in the tundra with nothing but rage and a desire to eat meat is worth your hideous physique.

Otogi 2 returned to the pastoral state of wonder that Otogi 1 could not achieve in its railway time table desire for you to get an S rank.

From the moment you stepped into a strange forest in Otogi 1, you wondered what you were doing there. Sure, it was immediately fascinating, but as it progressed, it became merely frustrating. If I do not understand what is going on, I want to explore. If I cannot explore, then I am sad.

Otogi 2 dispensed with ambiguity and the hint of environments which were not locked down, and simply conveyed the straight ahead vision of the conductor. This train (by which I mean dashing through space and tearing things apart with a sword) is all I do. I will do it well. If the mission is on a ship sailing through a sea of the dead, and there are spirits assailing it, then I will leap off my ship and destroy them. If the only anchor to the world — my mysterious female . . . guide-mission-narrator (person) — tells me that a fox god is trying to destroy humanity (because they are tainted by demons, and our inherent capacity for darkness as well as light leads to all suffering), then I know that, eventually, I will be slicing through the sky, straight as an arrow, toward that spirit. No matter what happens, I have a destiny to destroy it.

It is not a matter of collecting the most points, or trying to achieve the best possible score. If you are scored, then it’s a matter of assessing your performance in order to help you achieve an improvement. If you didn’t want to improve, then why would you play? Conversely, if you wanted to play at all, why wouldn’t you want to improve?

Otogi 2 is the achievement of the Achievement system, without its OCD-like quality of The Mountain (which was climbed Because It Was There). Every achievement in Otogi is a mastery of the character, and a chance to tune its performance. Indeed, it is the possibility of taking another character, and mastering that level in an entirely different way. Otogi 2 is a fighting game in which an entire level is your opponent. Sometimes this is personified in a singular superghost or challenge, but far more often, the level is arranged with ploys — tactics it will use to unseat you, and bring you low. To defeat it, you must remain nimble.

The bridge between Otogi 1 and Otogi 2 is possibly Gun Valkyrie. While what is charming in Otogi 2 was odious in the first game, Gun Valkyrie was charming and odious as often as it could be over and over again, very quickly, until it looked like the robin was inside the cage, due to a phenomenon known as “optical illusion” (hushed noises of awe are appropriate). There was some discussion around its release that GunValk was a difficult game. I suppose it might be if your brain does not function in the way that the game demands it function; specifically, in GunValk, the Enemy Gate is always Down. If you can reorient your point of view as needed to treat the levels as Space Harrier like continuous fields — fields which should be filled with plasma, then flitting about spastically while unleashing firepower should be no great burden (except in a specific way that makes this all relevant soon to be revealed). Even if you decided to grasp the brass ring of a right-brain-only task by utilizing the other character, who could not flit and fight effectively at the same time, the game never stopped feeling like an open world in which to explore survival as kind of punctuation mark on the end of pure movement for its own joyous sake. However, if your brain doesn’t want to or like to operate in this way, then it is certainly difficult, and you may very well be better suited to something like Ninja Gaiden, where baskets of structure waft beneath you to catch you should you fall into a trap of too much expression for its own sake. Performing those somersaults with no clear goal in mind is a burden — you need enemies. You need hard-as-balls enemies to give you structure. So that you do not accidentally fly into their midst during one of your glorious dances and end them without realizing you just Did Something Difficult, Ninja Gaiden will always emphasize how close you are to getting your shit kicked in by demon dogs in a filthy alley that might be the toilet of some ancient evil (for a good time call *choking noise mixed with sneezing leopards on fire*).

GunValk supplemented these moments of amazing freedom to achieve, with hellaciously poorly planned navigation through a sprawling level. To wit: the first level involves walking all the way to one end of a canyon, and then walking back out. Enemies have been created to make sure it is, almost literally (but not quite), uphill both ways naked in the snow. With a heavy gun. And no upgrades. It’s not difficult, it’s just that you can fly, for chrissake, and you are stuck doing this by pressing down on the joystick while pressing forward — losing altitude at the end of every scoot because your thumb is tired and the controller was never designed for such hideous idiocy. It’s fine when you are moving back and forth, but has no conception of a forth-centric world. If the designers could not determine how to allow you the freedom of movement to get where you wished to go *and* make the deadly dance of the garden shears amongst the flowers, then they should have kept their levels smaller or the travel parts automated in some way (as if you intend to go anywhere else but into and out of that canyon). I’m sure you’ll agree, during the level where you fly all over a large pit filled with fungi, desperately jumping and jet-gliding from one giant mushroom to another, as though chiseling crampons into the ice as you scale Everest, one decimeter at a time (Because It’s There, oddly enough–and keep in mind that might not be what a crampon is–so whatever, just use the right term there and pretend I’ve climbed an ice gorged mountain near the ceiling of heaven before), the idea of using these controls to get somewhere is a sin on par with cannibalism. There is a level where you do nothing but fall (for instance, towards an enemy gate), and must choose when to arrest your fall and dispense wrath. This is when the game is greatest.

In Otogi 1, everything is about a panicked rush from one area to another, with means of conveyance which are at odds with the demands of the level. You do not have time for this shit, and every time you try to move, you shorten your time. So, to get some more on the clock, you must heal-toe the gas and break as you round turn 3, striving to ethereate some ghosts who form the orange cones by the roadside, in time to get the health you need to continue onward, without incurring the wrath of Red Fire or the even worse Blue Fire (is so *fire*. . . it’s BLUE; can you believe that shit?). Otogi is not generally about getting from place to place. It is, generally, about rushing through space, like the runner from Marathon spitting out his report of victory over the forces of Persia, before punctuating that sentence with Death. Only, in the game, it should be the death of whatever they have on hand, rather than your own (which is more often the case).


In Otogi 2, some rational, caring individual realized that, in a world of short, pointed sentences, with strong verbs taking direct objects to be theirs, until death do they part, after the period, or very occasionally, the Bang (exclamation), the text field for entering these dramatic statements should be precisely as wide as the statements themselves, and suffer no further information to be written.

At first, this seems like a limitation, until you realize, like the reaffirmed Tokyo bullet train conductor, you are free; free from the Volksmarch timepiece which judges your every failure, and instead, you are pursuing the 1000 perfect teacups of success. The train can go faster. It can decelerate more smoothly. It can use less energy. It can be cleaner. I can arrive at work and get my coffee with more time to spare. All these things and more can be mine — and I will have them if I want them. My Bento will always be there, but if my day is going well, I may forget it. My wife will understand — she knows that I love my work and take it very seriously.

Even though I am just a conductor.

Otogi 2 will make you love the idiocy that is “side scrolling beat ‘em up” (which is what this genre used to be called before kids on their skateboards used the Interweb to email their cellphones a 3rd dimension and a bunch of fancy pants vectors, specular maps, and whatnot: get off my lawn). Double Dragon was about efficiency, and in its amazingly obtuse “Vs.” mode, that’s all there was to do — be efficient. While DD was fun, and a great cache of memories, it was not amazing. What’s amazing is Otogi 2, which can make you feel like every limitation is a wonderful freedom to concentrate on the only thing that matters:

The Fox Spirit must die.

–Action Button Dot Net
