Dawn of War II

21Apr2009

a review of
Dawn of War II
a videogame developed by Relic entertainment
and published by THQ
text by samuel kite

2 stars

Bottom line: Dawn of War II is “brand poisoning”.


Games Workshop’s Warhammer 40k universe is, allegedly, different than its Warhammer Fantasy universe, but could be the same thing if someone just had the balls to arm all the elves with wands and get a dragon to drop them off at school.

In the interests of full disclosure, as a teenager, I was very much a fan and participant in Games Workshop’s (GW) many ploys to rip off young people. I have piles of lead and plastic in boxes which are waiting for the day when I have children and I need to find a way to keep them indoors away from influences like drugs, gangs, or healthy human socialization. When I started playing the ‘game’ Warhammer 40k (sarcastic quotes meant to imply that the only game you’re playing is some kind of metagame involving ways to spend money efficiently on arbitrarily overpriced bullshit whilst discovering a strange commonality with 1st generation American immigrants from Eastern Europe who, as often as they could, patched their clothes, cobbled together semi-necessities out of plucky knowhow and the castoffs of the middle class, to construct for themselves such luxuries as spoons, cutlery, and tampons made from dryer lint, but in a vaguely artistic way, which I suppose makes it all ok), it was focused on a very detailed level of strategy where the rules that forced you to move and position your pieces in particular ways determined, to a certain extent, victory. This is what you might expect from a board ‘game’ (this time, the quotes are signalling that the term isn’t ironic — however, I’m hearkening back to the previous sentence which was *totally* ironic). There was a certain degree of Magic the Gatheringesque deck building in the form of selecting your units, and equipping them, but in many ways, these rules existed simply to accommodate the tinkering urge the game professed to nurture–that of hacking up, and sculpting together your toys from spare parts (or altogether from scratch if you were particularly ambitious).

However, the design of the game changed as various editions were released, until your input on the table was largely academic. The outcome was determined by a conspiracy of luck. Luck in the choices you made to build your army, luck in the (many many) rolls of the dice, and luck in the various random scenarios and objectives which were valuable as part of victory conditions. The game was not the focus of the hobby, it was one arm of a multilevel marketing scheme by and for nerds (or those too innocent to realize they were being nerded).

GW’s business model revolves around 3 central properties and a 3rd ‘slot’ filled by whatever project they happen to be in the mood to produce (mood determined by rolling 2d6 and consulting chart). The central properties are Warhammer Fantasy, their fairly typical amalgam of Tolkien, Verne, and Arthurian fantasy tropes (with, over time, additions from the renaissance, strange visions of mayan fantasy, and just about anything else ever written in paperback form by someone with nothing interesting to say–e.g. R. A. Salvatore), Warhammer 40k (standing for the year 40,000 AD, as measured by Space Catholics), an initially superficial transportation of all their fantasy concepts in a somewhat gritty far flung future based on Herbert, Verne (again), Giger, or whatever they saw at the theater that month–which later grew, thanks to the fantastic and prolific contributions of John Blanche (commercial painter extraordinaire, who’s untainted voice shines through more clearly in his work than even the likes of Brom or Craig Mullins–a glorious achievement for a man who’s work is seen and consumed without being hopelessly deformed (at least I hope that’s what’s going on, because I like it (it’s like Giger without the obscurity, though, I guess, he’s substantially more obscure)) into a legitimately different flavorful cornucopia of dark visions reaching so far forward into the future of genuine scifi philosophy, that it curves back upon the past (as the best scifi does), and shows us a convincing next rung on the ladder of the slow, uncertain future of our species, and White Dwarf, their shill mag (which, as shill mags go, beats the everloving shit out of Official Xbox magazine). The remaining slot is filled by any number of products at a given time: Epic 40k (15mm scale version of the normally 25mm Warhammer 40k), Warmaster (15mm scale version of the normally 25mm Warhammer Fantasy), Blood Bowl (football with elves), Space Hulk (football with space men–and before a die hard fan attempts to deny me, think about it for 5 seconds), Mordheim (a squad based, somewhat RPGish version of Warhammer Fantasy), Necromunda (a squad based, somewhat RPGish version of Warhammer 40k), Battlefleet Gothic (a naval engagement game set in the Warhammer 40k universe), Man o War (a naval engagement game set in the Warhammer Fantasy universe), Gorka Morka (a mad-max style vehicle focused game revolving around a particular planet of Orks in the Warhammer 40k universe), and Dragon Princes of Ulthuan (a mad-max style vehicle focused game revolving around the conflict between the high elves and dark elves set in the Warhammer Fantasy universe–also something I just now made up, but you get my point). Usually one of these products is Just coming Out (holy shit!) and one of them is experiencing some kind of expansion cycle, while the rest languish on the back burners, turning tricks for spare cash from whoever is accidentally ensnared by reading an old issue of the shill mag, or finding some surplus stock at their local hobby shop. The rotation continues to reposition the market in order to shake the cash out of people’s pockets. This is a Business Model. For that reason, you’ve never seen a half decent video game about Warhammer 40k until a few years ago, when GW made the mistake of hiring Relic Entertainment to make an RTS based on their table top franchise.

Now, you could say that I’m a conspiracy nut for relating that, once upon a time, when people (fans) asked ‘why don’t you make an RTS out of this property–it’d be really great’, the company’s reason was that any video game which made good use of the property would deprive them of their business model, because you’d only have to buy the game once, and the game would give you infinite imaginary soldiers to play with (part of the understandable ‘video games will destroy all other forms of play behavior, and by extension, toys’ concept that made the rounds until it turned out that people still like toys). You *could* say that, only the editor of the shill mag actually answered a mailbag question of this stripe with the condescending and obnoxious reply ‘Well, then what reasons would people have to keep playing the tabletop game!? *chortle*’. For this reason, GW licensed games were, for a long time, a joke. They usually consisted of properties which were ‘off cycle’ in their endless churn of ‘new releases’, and, even then, included bizarre reinterpretations of boardgame rules which were, in some case, physically impossible to play (Space Hulk for PC (1993)–try it out–you’ll be amazed at how impossible it is). Blizzard eventually humbled GW by making more money off the conceptual meat of their products than GW has ever, itself, made off its own poisonous approach to the finances of the young (and keep in mind, when I say ‘young’, there is a large following of people who sink much more significant dollar amounts into the hobby, that are actual adults–many of them married, but you have to understand that these people are not the tax base for the empire–they are an upper class, of sorts–the real money is made in 10s and 20s one at a time, slowly, from millions of children, not from the 10s of thousands of adults who occasionally drop 200 to 500 dollars–or pounds, since it’s a british company). Having seen that the cat was out of the bag, and that it was covered in golden fur, GW wised up and started hunting for people to bring its nearly unvarnished product to the computer. From early attempts like Shadow of the Horned Rat (pretty alright), to Chaos Gate (mostly not bad), to the more recent Mark of Chaos (more dissimulation goes here), and the first Dawn of War–as soon as they hit the ground with the actual board game concept legitimately reproduced (in real time, at any rate, with the economics of the hobby stripped out), they had something much more closely resembling a great game (which you could have already inferred from the success of Warcraft and Starcraft). What is the point of all this exposition? I’m not sure. Bear with me.

So there’s this long history, and Relic, who have never produced a bad game (yes, even Impossible Creatures–not liking something doesn’t make it bad) was finally put in charge of the franchise that I held nostalgia for–it seemed like the rapture was upon us (in case of etc this player will be afk (god, I hate self satisfied bumper stickers)). But when they actually put the game out and its subsequent 4 expansions covering nearly all the armies not originally included, you could see, finally, the wheels turning on that hideous business model again. All in all, I’ve sunk about as much money into Dawn of War as I have into the final fantasy series over my entire lifetime–including a couple months of Final Fantasy XI (I… I don’t like to talk about it).

But, despite all that, there is a key component missing from the game in the GW plan to destroy us all, which is the toys themselves. On the tabletop, you have no choice but to fetishize these objects you’ve sunk so much time and money into. You’ve painted them. Often, you’ve sculpted them–at the very least you assembled them. When Little Big Planet finally came out (despite nearly causing muslims to destroy Japan in a flurry of self annihilation), most people who think about it at all, think ‘huh… that seems a little thin’, but if you ever actually see the charming characters, possibly wearing a zebra head or something, and just look at all that cavorting for a few minutes, you fall in love with the idea of making something in that world. Obviously, you aren’t actually going to go out and do it.

It’s a Guitar Hero for game design, going through the motions is a better cost-benefit ratio for most sauri than working yourself into knots just to find out you’re mediocre.

Just because some random person made Zelda in LBP doesn’t mean that it is nurturing an independent creative ambition that will actually result in action in the normal test subject, precisely the same way that Spore is not making scientists (or… priests. I’m honestly not sure what their thesis is, there). Rational people don’t screw around with menus endlessly making triggers so that a triceratops can propose marriage to their special muffin when a heart sticker is affixed to its crotch. In Dawn of War, your troops are utterly expendable. In the campaign, where they are *less* expendable (since you accumulate special units which you use to rush the enemy’s position at the beginning of every map and win by default), they are still pretty anonymous. What this means is that you get to enjoy the game part of the ‘game’ (which, under Relic’s ministrations, has far more meat than the tabletop version). You could conceivably play Dawn of War or, for that matter, Company of Heroes over and over for years and be perfectly happy, without ever getting any expansions. Dawn of War II, on the other hand, is probably meant to be LBP for tabletop wargaming, but ends up serving that function for MMOs (as a player, not as a creator–the best way to simulate creating an mmo is to download a recipe for meth and start cooking).

GW pioneered the MMO before there was such a concept. Which is unsurprising; they started life as an RPG company. RPGs, in pen and paper form, existed solely to provide a structure which helped people tell an interactive story. To the extent that some kind of ‘game’ was involved, it could be a product of people’s own deviousness, like whist or poker (and I say deviousness with a twinkle in my eye and a grin–the kind of wily charm that makes man superior company to monkey). The part where you accumulate abstract points and roll dice is nothing more than a way to pass time and provide a common context, not a game unto itself. When the transition to the computer was made, the ‘RPG’ continued to hold sway based around the idea that, often, a software engineer had a story to tell but no mechanics to go with it. It’s relatively easy to create a surrogate for pacing and purpose with levels and monsters. GW had a more secure grip on this concept earlier in their development: the fact of the matter is, sadly, people are not generally good story tellers. The RPG community suffers from a severe shortcoming–that, while dungeons and dragons *sounds* really fun and intriguing, you have to play it with other dungeons and dragons players. It’s a 0 sum situation. Don’t play, and never enjoy it, or, do play, with other people fumbling at the poor thing’s undergarments while it writhes and attempts to escape, and be haunted by an agonizing personal guilt for having participated in sordid excess and buggery. Sometimes it’s not that bad, but when it comes to *kids* at least, the ones who are manifestly in a position to try out these play behaviors, since they lack superior options such as fornication or alcohol, it’s generally not pretty. At least when (if) there are more concrete rules rather than ‘tell a story’, you can avoid some of the petty arguments that can occur from being a simmering kettle of hormones (I shot you! Nu Uh!). Point is, GW understands that exploiting the target audience *means* bringing together toys and tots in a structured environment, while replacing the an abstract set of mechanics with a very real financial system.

In DnD, the ‘game’ is telling a story the mechanics are a psychological negotiation that encompasses the same challenges and rewards in having an interesting conversation (with other people). The Rules are the motivation to continue and maintain continuity, and could as easily be replaced with a book club, the superbowl, or, even, yo momma. In Warhammer, the ‘game’ is an actual (borderline) game, to be played with the toys that you purchase on a continuing basis–those purchases are your motivation and continuity, the premise upon which discussions with other enthusiasts revolve. You may beat your friend a number of weeks running because you have purchased units which are effective against his. Next week, he may purchase a counter. Eventually, you may avoid fighting each other altogether, because you know the outcome ahead of time–you both bought things which *looked* good to you at the time, it just turns out that his are better in the arbitrary structure of the game at that moment. They are intriguing toys, in any case, making the bitter pill relatively easy to swallow.

In the end, the crapshoot of the highly luck-based table top game will pan out similarly over time, even with the endless adjustments and rereleases planned by the company. Why bother facing his demon guy if you know that the only way for your commander guy to win is if the demon guy misrolls over and over again? In some iterations of the game, the mechanics revolved around rerolling, and preventing the actions of an opponent with special cards or abilities so much that, in the mandated 6 turn limit, no amount of strategy could produce the least bit of certainty regarding the outcome. But the company is not concerned with challenge or giving a best-fit-path of mechanics (implying a system in which mechanics exist to be manipulated by a player), their concern is providing a structure in which everyone can continue the conversation for as long as possible, given the same premise (as previously discussed money –> GW). Balancing the game back and forth such that the toys have no static, determinable value from month to month is what the game designers are there to do.

This is all fine by the company, because the fundamental lack of decisive outcomes in the board game encourage multiple purchases. Specifically, the idea of purchasing units, painting them, and assembling them, until you have a ‘complete’ army with various options that allow you to deploy a ‘strategically sound’ force in a given scenario is a complete pipedream for the average kid (even beyond the notion of anything resembling strategy being at the mercy of hugely aberrant spikes in randomness). It’s the orgasm of ‘level up’ you never attain. Even if you have the money to get all the toys, you probably will lose interest before you finish painting them. That sense of being ‘finished’ is the carrot on the stick. If you are an adult who still maintains interest in the hobby, you experience these orgasms on an ongoing basis–for you, the endless falsehood of ‘new releases’ is what keeps you tentatively aroused ad infinitum (possibly making repeated false starts on armies which are never ‘completed’–no doubt by subconscious design). Until you die alone, I guess. I’m not sure what the ‘endgame’ is like for the hobby. In general, people get eventually disgusted with the hobby, or else get a girlfriend or wife, and she gets disgusted, and the whole thing goes by the wayside. This more than anything identifies its similarity with the MMO.

What GW really wants is an Idolmaster style insanity realized in a game form. Dawn of War II seems like a step in that direction.

Dawn of War II, taking a page from (I guess?) Warcraft 3, fetishizes every unit to the upteenth degree in the single player campaign. You accomplish something very similar to the campaign map of the original(s), but, rather than dropping in a base, with your commander unit and vanguard of elite (earned) special squads, implying the possibility of special scenarios where the *base* you build, and the *plan* you put in place is the only way to stop the onslaught of the enemy who’s doing something similar, there is only your commander and 3 hand-picked squads. In the multiplayer game of the original, executing on a plan–even given its Starcraft veneer of fighting game sensibilities (in a flaccid, passive way–though, admittedly, when an eldar warlock sends space marines soaring through the air after utilizing the psychic power ‘barking spider’, that’s a *much* better step toward feeling something when you play than having something say ‘stop clicking me’ after you click it for the 40th time in a row–for no other purpose than to find out what it has to say when its repeatedly clicked) it is genuinely fun. The key feeling of vibration you desire in RTS play, that you are progressing and becoming a better opponent even when you’re trampled underfoot, is in the original game. While it can be formulaic (I defy you to survive against a good player’s commander without building a turret, first thing), most games tend to be, and there is enough wiggle room between the armies to make the whole thing satisfying. In Dawn of War II, the multiplayer game is still intensely personal, and, to the extent that there’s any plan, that plan has more to do with the units you picked, and how you accesorize them, rather than, well, anything else, really.

In Warcraft III, admittedly, there is less personal agency in the selection of the units. One of the reasons for that is that the units are all individuals, and many of them are hard to control *as* individuals. The logistics of the game are top-heavy (especially in the utterly insane outgrowth Defense of the Ancients). Another reason is that the units are not cultivated, but for the commanders. In fact, the units can end up screwing you, if they take resources away from your commander. In Dawn of War II, there aren’t any logistics anymore, but then, there aren’t many characters either. The mechanics are all individualistic, but there are still squads. You end up playing a real time version of Xcom. Xcom, as it turns out, without any time to prepare a strategy or build your unit into the squad that *you* want to see on the battlefield, is fairly cumbersome.

In order to play the game in the campaign, you have to identify key attributes of the various pieces of junk they keep dumping in your lap and determine the best way to keep yourself from getting bored. For instance, your commander can get a shield which will prevent all damage until he runs out of energy. Given a commander with alot of health, and the right kinds of corollary equipment, you can send him, all alone, into the next marked objective on the map, and watch him succeed–except for bizarre, hard to identify circumstances where some combination of enemy weapons is making his shield go away very quickly. It’s nearly impossible to tell what will kill him ahead of time–you just send him in to see. The entire behavior of units varies wildly based on some of the equipment that drops, but the drops of equipment are not random (or don’t appear to be). You will receive your first sniper rifle on the 3rd level (or whatever–I forget when I got it–you get the idea), and thereafter, your unit-capable-of-sniping can snipe. Assuming you put enough points into ranged combat to get the snipe ability. But his two cohorts in the squad don’t get the same weapon, so they are relegated to peppering the enemy with machine gun fire. Why even have the other guys there at all? They exist only as attributes of the character. This is all training you to understand the multiplayer game (while serving double duty as an end-in-itself). When you pump out a unit-capable-of-sniping in the game, what you need to do is focus on nurturing its success, such that you have the resources, and it has the experience to become an actual sniping unit. Once this is done, it has to be nurtured again, to make sure that you keep your sniping unit. In fact, you might want to hide it in a building somewhere–it’s far to valuable to actually put in a threatening position on the field.

Which isn’t to say it encourage cowardice, just that it discourages new production, which is the financial equivalent to cowardice.

As an MMO engine, Dawn of War II is nearly perfect. I could envision playing with 2 or 3 squads for 80 ‘levels’ of some imaginary scifi mmo. The ‘pvp’ (which in any other game is called ‘multiplayer’) would be amazing. On the other hand, it’s not a real game, because, when you tell, for instance, your commander to charge to a point on the ground (through walls, which has a hilarious mentally handicapped kind of feel to it–though I guess the idea is to evoke the terminator, not the offspring of incest), he might be busy. He could be hitting Some Guy, or maybe he’s Turning Around In Place to do whatever, or maybe he’s Thinking About Daffodils. He *might* charge to that point, or he might take a few steps and then do it. He might charge 2 centimeters into a rock on the ground that, for whatever reason, has more mass and cohesion than a tank trap or brick wall, and stop suddenly (you still have to wait for the charge ability to recharge after this–despite having accomplished nothing). If you order him to charge outside his maximum charge range, he might wait until he’s close enough before he tries to use the ability. Or not! He might also forget what he was doing once he gets there, which complicates things. To further confuse the issue, he has his own mini-charge he uses often that isn’t the same, but involves the kind of basic common sense like avoiding obstacles which the larger charge does not. When you tell your jump troops (troops with jet packs that help them reposition at a distance quickly, and disrupt the enemy when they land on them) to jump, there’s not just one of them, so one of them might jump immediately, then another may jump a second later, and the third may have to get up, finish picking his nose, wash his hands, and *then* jump. Ironically, the lack of immediacy in *that* circumstance is usually a huge boon, since each trooper does roughly the same amount of disruptive damage to the enemy when landing, but the enemy can’t be disrupted more than once per wild hilarious flailing prat-fall. If you could train these guys to stagger their jumps, you’d be able to keep the enemy off balance much longer. In general, controlling the squad deserves tighter controls, or else looser mechanics. Here, the compromise has gone in favor of unit identity, rather than responsiveness.

Which is what it comes down to. Company of Heroes is a very impressive RTS adaptation of a serious war game. Part of the reason it succeeds is that cover is *real*. It doesn’t just disappear every time a thyroid gland wanders through it. The real impact of taking a minimally maneuverable piece of WWII armor and crashing through a hedgerow is full realized. The stilted but deadly nature of the equipment on the battlefield during that era comes across accurately, and, what’s more, brings to the game the very real and interesting problems that they weapons were designed to overcome. Real life balanced Company of Heroes long before Relic ever took a stab at it. Their contribution, more than anything else, was tweaking the numbers on the simulation. Dawn of War (the original and expansions) is not real, and is not meant to be real. The free flow of the nodes in its system are designed to work around the limitations of a practical wargame meant to be played For Fun in your Spare Time. The fact that someone shoots a missile through the ground to hit a walker standing past some defelade is fine. The point isn’t that it was in cover, the point is that it is a robot, and you have to shoot it with missiles. Ownership of the unit isn’t as important as the capabilities of the unit. It is a tool to be used. In 2, ownership is the entire point. The strategy is a coherent approach to improvement of that ownership, which, at least, starts out in a pristine state of virginity at the beginning of every match (though, I’m sure, those player win/loss record rankings are just aching to get transformed into a call of duty style ranking system that translates to the units themselves).

In Dawn of War II, all the detail exists in an impossibly epic universe. Stand in cover? These men wear armor that can repel the fire of a tank. They do so while not wearing a helmet. They carry on their backs weapons which, according to the story, at least, can burn holes with fire from the center of a sun through forcefields sheathing imaginary compounds that are harder than any combination of comic book abs and adolescent erections. This universe is not real. It was never vetted for realism, and it shouldn’t have to be. The game itself was never even meant to be a great game–it is a way to play with toys in such a fashion that any arguments which might break out can be refereed. There are all kinds of ways in which what happens on a regular basis on the tabletop makes no rational sense–even given the irrational circumstances under which disbelief is suspended. So, to transport it into this realm where ‘men take cover’ and that ‘means something’ is, itself, irrational. It certainly seems like it might make sense on the surface, but really, it prices itself out of the game very quickly. The only thing it does well is, on a purely aesthetic level, help to fetishize the toys–since they’re acting all secret-squirrel crouching down behind paper-mache which will disappear in a puff of fucking smoke as soon as some hideous creature leaps toward it. I suppose it’s a tool to help you understand where your squads will stand when you tell them to move to a position, but considering how quickly battle forces them to fall all over the place into disarray, this particular feature seems like a lie. It lies to you. In fact, if anything, this game is missing a key feature of the old tabletop editions; any troops out of formation *have* to move up *into* formation. Why? Because otherwise it’s just an endless clusterfuck.

Which underscores a key point about that board game. While ‘cover’ has become in vogue in the table top (I say in vogue, because there were times when it was meaningless) as a new form of ‘saving throw’ you can roll, it is usually inferior to the armor the soldier is wearing. In almost all cases, it is meaningless, except as a means to slow you down. The video game has too much emphasis on this throw-away form of safety that the tabletop acknowledges is just for show, to try to make the plastic aquarium plants you bought seem more meaningful (as if imaginary ferns could stop an imaginary laser meant to destroy imaginary super tanks–at that point, why not just put your waffles down as cover and fight over the pepper shaker), but make little to no efforts to emphasize maneuver, and the limitations upon it. I can understand that, certainly, and in some ways, an RTS can’t stand too much complication in the details of getting from place to place, but this is why other genres of games exist in which to showcase personable iconic characters.

Dawn of War II is the first time I think I’ve seen Relic learn the wrong lessons from their products. It is brand poisoning. If the next Company of Heroes had dropped all the base building pretense in order to bring a modern-warfare flavored version of itself, the changes to focusing on territory and unit management with the industry side of things taken wholly off-screen would have been a relevant streamlining. Instead, this is a backward step into an Xcom Apocalypse or Fallout 2 (real time mode). When I play this game, what I want is *more* concessions to the needs of a game, and less concessions to some kind of ‘realism’ in a far flung future of fancy–a realism that whose only engineered objective is to increase the randomness of an outcome to string people along and keep them in the context.

Then there’s the inevitable fact that the expansions *will* come. When they do, this thing is going to turn even more into a DotA like experience than it already is (and it isn’t *quite*–for one thing, the characters are all under your control, instead of being shitty ice skating nodes that affect the arbitrary and automated behaviors of cannonfodder while navigating byzantine upgrade paths through equipment pickups and a leveling system that are a product of at least 40 teenaged syntheses of Every Fantasy Novel They’ve Read That Summer). Considering that DemiGod is the offering from Chris “Last Great Hope of the RTS Genre, (note the emphasis on Strategy)” Taylor’s company, after Supreme Commander took the concept of the commander unit and turned the base building outgrowth into a personalized, overly detailed pokemon-like construct, where you were in charge of all the automation that DotA does on its own–but didn’t get much out of that participation (other than memorizing an invisible chart somewhere inside you where grass type counters medium range ballistic missile type), it’s no surprise that the other great RTS company, Relic, is also moving down the gullet of Geppetto’s whale… of warcraft.

This is the cynical destruction of all good game genres by the hamster wheel. Like some kind of nuclear secret, the overt use of conditioning mechanisms has broken open game design and become a strange end in itself. As if moving pictures on a screen which you control is not enough incentive for participation; now the only way to snare the populace is to find ways to get them to spin their wheels accumulating imaginary points–points which *anyone* can get, because they come in the form of candy that falls out of a pinata. If you can’t hit the thing with the stick yourself, an adult will push you in the right direction. Get the candy kids! That’s what we’re here for.

At least Nintendo, damn their souls forever, is using this mindlessness to achieve some good. While Wii Fit is, in some ways, an abomination, it, at least, harnesses these unfair hooks into the psyche to try to convince someone to get in better shape. The only problem is that it is the bird-like shape of a japanese person, which, to a normal land mammal, is an unhealthy state of near death. Did xbox live’s achievement system divert this unhealthy urge in a way that allows games to exist and avoid the urge to consume? Apparently not, since, after logging into steam to play Dawn of War II, the game logs itself into Xbox live, and my screen gets come all over its face from two different shells running in the background. Now I’m not only getting steam stats on the game, I’m getting XBL achievements (Massacre! – 10 points… what the fuck does that mean?), *and* the characters are Actually leveling up in the game and, in case that wasn’t enough, they’re getting better, rarer item drops from monsters, and if *that* is still not enough, I can perform optional side missions to raise my ‘Savior’ and ‘Warrior’ rankings in the campaign.

I’m so exhausted I think I need to masturbate.

Addendum: The cinematic intro for Dawn of War II is laughably bad in comparison to the intro to Dawn of War. Specifically, the first game’s intro captured all of the oddball circumstances of the background without ever feeling silly. Obviously, what was happening was super serious, and no moment had awkward movements. In this short, every second is filled with the most juvenile fantasy of an action movie that, at the 2 points when it manages comic timing (probably by accident), it’s almost a relief. You may not notice the painful deadspot in motion when the commander is doing some faux samurai ‘I have cleaved you’ pose while the farseer thinks about cherry blossoms and falls on her back like a drunk, even though it has none of the corollary presentation to make it work, but if you don’t, it’s only because, in comparison to the scene earlier where he’s running from a couple shrieking girls with swords and you can practically hear yakkity sax’s impramatur of zaniness, or even earlier, when a gunner does a ‘where’d he go’ moment before getting shot and stabbed, it’s almost competent (note: It is not actually competent). When the final scene starts, and the commander flexs his fingers, firmly gripping the shaft of his chainsallus, and makes a special olympics highlight reel quality run into the foreground (awkward, but emphatic), I was completely disgusted.     But then I thought about it some more. Most of these worlds try to be ‘always interesting’, and generate content the way I described, making it actually always boring. But some of them say to hell with it, and use complex algorithms that most of the time create crap, and only very rarely make something *really* interesting. So what we need is a very complex set of algorithms for generation, to guarantee incredible variety (also guaranteeing incredible crap most of the time), and a very robust set of exploration mechanics for players to use.

    Let’s make it flying and portals. You buy yourself a flying beast, mount it, and launch on your merry way. You cover and see a lot of dull terrain in a short time, and then come across something interesting. A meadow, glacier, hill, whatever. It’s really pretty and potentially has exploitable resources. You land, pick a nice spot, and create a permanent portal to a settlement of your choice (or maybe the one you just came from). Anyone over there can see your pretty meadow through the portal, and decide to pass through for a nominal fee. You get the fee. So if your spot is really pretty and attracts people you will be rewarded with hard cash (of course, creating the portal itself is quite expensive, so you can’t just plop them all over the place and hope for the best). You fly on in search of the next pretty location.
    Note that 95% of the players will never bother with this; they will simply use the network of created portals to travel all over the place, and see only the best that our huge world has to offer. But the 5% of explorers will serve as human filters for our dynamic content, and ensure that we get the most of it (and be handsomely paid for it in-game). All of this can be applied to dynamically created weapons, enemies, whatever.

    So user-discovered content is I think actually a pretty good idea, as long as you minimize the number of players exposed to the dull stuff, maximize exposure to the cool stuff, and make it all intrinsic to the game.

    Am I onto something here? Comments please.

What is was missing was the Dungeons and Dragons Online aspect of really coming to grips (literally) with the world through ledges, ladders, and steps. There need to be ropes too.

    From the standpoint of network gaming, predictable physics make life easier, not harder. What really kills the experience has nothing to do with a technical implementation–after all, if the server needs to know your position, it doesn’t matter how you got there, or what you were doing–you still need to tell it that position. What’s killing it is an archaic and sociall backward concept of ‘cheating’ being the worst of all sins that can occur in a game world. So, physics, which, by nature, are manipulable, and give the player the potential to do things which aren’t ‘designed’ (because design is thought of in a backwards way by game designers who think they can make a system conform to their expectations by bludgeoning it with special cases until it falls in line) are out the window for player movement. For one thing, if you have to give the physics leeway to interpret the player’s position on the client, then you open the possibility of writing a cheat that lets someone move at any speed–since the server isn’t in a position to check on anything but their position.

    Plus there’s a resources problem. Good motion is hard. It’s harder than music or 3d or 2d art. The practitioners of animation are fewer and further between than ‘traditional’ artists that work in the digital space. Because it’s hard to define motion’s impact on design and player satisfaction, most companies simply assume it can be done as an afterthought by 1 or 2 people on the team with any inclination to perform the task. The resources are rather put into bullet point things like number of levels, variety of enemies, and other raw assets that can be tabulated and enumerated. Nevermind that the artistic effort in 3 halfassed levels could be put into one really great moving enemy and protagonist that, together, can fill any number of anonymous arenas and be great–that is a long bet, without the certainty of something more measurable. A texture is a tangible thing. A fully articulated character just seems like good character art–people don’t even know what they like about it. Mario’s a great example. As a character, he’s an unlikeable tool–but the character seems great, because he moves well and is responsive. Think of how nothing but jump behavior affected how you felt about the characters in Super Mario 2. That’s a big deal. But to quantify it is hard. Luigi isn’t just a higher jumper–he’s floatier. He has slower acceleration in mid-air, it seems like. The princess has a ‘glide’ like ability that almost anticipates the raccoon cap (even though it’s a different game with a mario skin, and not related to the series really). The characters themselves are the same, but the jumps make them play differently. How do you put that in a design document where an animator isn’t part of the design process?

    The weird thing is that the City of Heroes ‘designer’, who was, in many ways, a writer of creative fiction for roleplaying games, rather than a rigorous systems designer (jack emmert I think is his name) probably had alot to do with the inclusion of the amazing movement. But he didn’t know enough to demand basic tools for interaction in the environment. There also seems to have been technical limitations of some kind that had to do with being understaffed or something–it wasn’t clear. The animation in that game is amazing–the fact that they found animators who really knew their trade is an accident though. What other assumption can you reach when the environment is missing all the other hooks of movement that make mario 64 great–the tangible things in the environment that mario can latch on to, or fling himself off or away from.

    It’s not a matter of technical challenge in terms of development. It’s a matter of art and design having the vision to put together a game for the specific purpose of having fun without doing some *thing*, in a world where tasks drive development, rather than goals.
