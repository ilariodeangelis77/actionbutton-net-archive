X-Men Origins: Wolverine (***)

a review of
x-men origins: wolverine
a videogame by raven software
published by activision
for the sony playstation 2
text by samuel kite
score: (out of four)

Bottom line: X-Men Origins: Wolverine is “doing something right.”


I think the test of a game, in some sense, comes when it does something spectacularly stupid; how do you feel? In XOW (moo), when you’ve just started getting used to invisible shotgun wielding soldiers, and the wonderful way the meat just falls right off the bone when brutally torn apart, they decide that you’ve been poisoned by a strange combination of AIDS and pink-eye which take away two of the three things that make Wolverine Wolverine: his healing and his ability to sense invisible jackasses by odor (third thing? He’s Canadian. Duh). In sequences that follow, what you’d expect is frustrating bullshit designed to irritate you. What it actually turns out to be is frustrating bullshit designed to remind you just how much fun you were having, and will subsequently have, when you get it all back (via bizarre medical torture device). What’s probably even more crazy is that you get your healing back before you get your commando-fart senses back, and this is the period when they throw the highest number of “shot gun in ur face, lul” moments at you.

Somehow, like the main character, it manages to hold together (against all odds (please conceal your boyish goosebumps or people will think you’re enjoying yourself (speaking of goosebumps, I used to get strange soothing shivers watching Mr. Rogers . . . Is that weird?))). In fact, everything works so well, that during the sequence where the game broke three times in a row (falling through the world twice, locked in place because the character failed to properly move during an in-game cinematic once), barely batting a (graceful, full-bodied) eyelash, I simply restarted from a check point. In fact, I spent about ten minutes killing four Wendigo over the top of a fence one had managed to fling me through, and still felt pretty good about restarting from checkpoint to destroy the helicopter that I couldn’t reach thereafter; trapped as I was by clipping failure (developer note: one-way clipping planes might be a peachy keen idea in some places . . . plus . . . a chain link fence? Seriously? That was the insurmountable barrier you chose to hem in that arena? What dark process occurs in the shriveled frontal lobes of a level designer that produce the invincible GTA3 trees or the Infamous/Wolverine chainlink fences?). The parenthetical has a point: the next time you’re thinking, “Wow, we have these great concrete barriers that some creatures can run right through,” take a quick mental inventory of the surroundings. If there is anything *less* sturdy than that which is somehow providing a context for the fight (a meaningless, irritating, archaic context that stinks of N64, even as it is inferior to it), fire the asshole who made it. Then murder his parents.

Don’t hire orphans.

The game really makes it on the basis of how much I enjoyed parts of it in spite of its faults. Any game which can make you feel like Jesus Christ (in terms of capacity for forgiveness) is doing something right. In this case, it’s the little touches. At the beginning of the game, you are shot out of a helicopter and sent plummeting toward earth with nothing much to do. Controlling your character at this point seems ridiculous, and, if you’re in the wrong mood, you’ll be starting on a froth of barely contained nerd outrage at the fact that you’re forced to watch while something stupid happens, powerless to stop it, but not powerless to writhe helplessly, which will call to mind for many the climactic death of Aeris in Final Fantasy VII, causing them to tear up as they savored the bittersweet memory of when they came closest to understanding what it’s like to love a woman as much as a wall-scroll of a juvenile catgirl in panties lapping milk out of a bowl that looks like Doraemon — and, wracked by this emotional turmoil, they’ll assume that the game has to be bad. This is the precise moment at which the game does the most horrible thing you’ve ever seen done to an imaginary person, and sets your expectations for the quote intensity level unquote at the right pitch of ‘gnaaaaaAAAaah’ (at the back of the throat . . . more of an “aaah” sound — there, you’ve got it).

Later, the game will be telling you about “quick kills.” These aren’t quite quick, and in some cases the term “kill” seems to be an understatement. For instance, in one animation, Wolverine will carefully separate all the major cuts from the carcass, save the tripe for stew, trim the fat and remove the bone; offering extremely reasonable prices on high quality grade A USDA approved ambiguous domestic combatant. Getting the damn things to work is like safe-cracking, at first. You grab your phở, and press the quick kill button. He kicks you in the face. You grab him again and press the slash button. You punch him a couple times, and then try the quick kill button again. He kicks you in the face. You pick him up, and throw him. He dies. You try another guy; he kicks you in the face. You then notice that you can “charge” the button a little by holding it down. You do so. Face-kicked. You pick up another asshat, and charge the button longer — Wolverine waits to a certain point and then drops the guy, and gets kicked in the face.

At this point, you’re wondering what this is all aboot?

The magic happens when you pick them up, charge the quick kill button until a flash appears on his claws (and a noise is made), and then press the slash button. When you finally get it, there’s almost no frustration, and afterwards, it feels like a little bit of foreplay and release every time you do it.

Another thing about the game that I like is you hardly ever find yourself having the early-morning brain conversation about your socks. For me, it usually follows a format along these lines. “Oh, look. I’m unfurling my socks to put them on and there’s a weird dark spec on one of them. I wonder what it is. Is it a bug?” The brain then launches the investigation. Arms are called, and they get ahold of fingers, and fingers touch the thing while you frown in concentration, ready to leap backwards sixty feet if it happens to be something dangerous like a dust mite or an aphid. “Nope, just a piece of dryer lint.”

But your brain is dumber than shit and won’t let it go. “Because if that were a bug, it’d be gross.” Right — but it’s not. “I wonder how a bug got into my socks, and how it would stay there.” It didn’t. There isn’t one. “Wouldn’t I have noticed it while I was rolling them up after I took them out of the dryer?” You can’t notice what wasn’t isn’t there, numbnuts. “I wonder what it was looking for in there.” It wasn’t looking for anything, you fat sack of worthless dead weight, it’s a god damned piece of dryer lint — stop fucking around with that concept and figure out how we’re going to get laid, today!

I have similar kinds of conversations with games on a fairly frequent basis, only instead of a bug that was just dryer lint, it’s Leaping Attack 43 A when I meant to Open The Door. In the most extreme cases, the game starts showing me the results of an enthusastic leap off a cliff when I Was trying to find out if a rock has a power-up behind it.

Wolverine’s control scheme never gets to be a pain because it’s segregated well. You don’t make those mistakes because there’s just enough of an effort put into making control inputs distinct that you’re not stuck in the Devil May Cry retarget-while-trying-to-move trap or the Metal Gear Solid punch-them-in-the-face-while-trying-to-snap-their-neck trap. That’s one of the things that makes it really enjoyable. Another is the merciful non-inclusion of unlockable moves. Yes, supermoves are time-released as you go through levels, and yes, you can spend experience on upgrading your abilities, but you’re never buying the basic moves you should have had all along (for instance, like Force Unleashed, where you unlock Move A on the ground, and then unlock Move A in the air, and then unlock Move A as a finisher on the ground, and etc., etc.). I upgraded my health first (for a total of three bubbles; not an endless journey to crazytown — also welcome), and my claws second, and then after that, anything else I chose was just a perk. I didn’t need something to survive or succeed — with health and damage, the basic play is fun and works.

I’m going to be moving on to Prototype and a stack of other crap pretty soon, here. I’ve already tried Street Fighter IV and wondered why the people who hate the Dead or Alive counter-anytime system are so in favor of the SF “mash buttons and counter accidentally” system, but, basically, I tackled Wolverine first because I assumed I’d have to do it early, while my patience was at its peak. Now I’m wondering if anything else will be this good.

Manage expectations. It’s the key to happiness, apparently. Also, simulated dismemberment.

–Action Button Dot Net
