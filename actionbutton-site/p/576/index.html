Overlord 2 (1/2*)

a review of
Overlord 2
a videogame developed by Triumph Studios
and published by Codemasters
text by Samuel Kite
score: 1/2 (out of four)

Bottom line: Overlord 2 is “[about] your perverted nesting instinct.”

1/2

The June issue of National Geographic has a headline on the front cover: “Pink Dolphins of the Amazon.” Obviously, that’s incredibly arousing. If you follow the link, the text below the image doesn’t help, either, since the second sentence reads, “Bending sinuous bodies, they glide through branches and curl like snakes around fluted trunks.”

I was passing by one of my harems, and while I was pointing out to the staff which one of the maidens was getting a little saggy, so that we could cook and eat her, it occurred to me that sex is pretty rough on everybody involved. There’s a great wildlife video (and here’s another one) of a candy weasel in heat out there (follow the related videos if you’re looking for more of the same) which seems to make a point which I can’t dispute. Love is hard on the grrls, u no?

Or maybe the point is that the phrase “bluffin’ with my muffin” should be about as hot sounding as an amazonian pink dolphin.

One of the reasons I play games is because I’m worried that, if I don’t, I’d end up becoming this one-dimensional player constantly thinking about hittin’ that and then quittin’ that. I’m sure most of us play games for the same reason. We’re a scourge on womankind, and we bottle up our dark gift, because it’s dangerous. Like, for instance, if we can somehow repress our urge to kill and eat our prey long enough to rape it, instead, we’d still want to kill and eat it after. We’re super vicious, is what I’m saying, we men.

Really, the only way to approach us is to castrate us with domestic distractions. That’s why everytime I blink, some muffin has put a throw pillow somewhere, or tried to put a doily under a vase. Think I’m crazy? Think this is just a conspiracy theory? No. We’re being dragged to IKEA one at a time to get castrated with allen wrenches and feng shui.

There are no girl minions. The main character can’t be a girl, either. I think if it could, it would probably stuff a minion down its shirt. On the other hand, you’ll end up feeling like one as you play (Minion or Girl? Yes).

This won’t make sense until I talk about the RTS on console.

When the RTS was invented, it was a turn based game that updated unit movement on a timer rather than by turns which attempted to simulate simultaneity. I think my favorite example was an Amiga game called Battlemech (or later Battlefort (page 62 on the viewer, page 60 in the magazine, left hand column about halfway down)) which was a transcription of the Battletech rules. This was strategy because the focus was on thinking through your actions, just that they carried out in a stack-based way, where the “quickest” happened before the slowest. It was possibly Dynamically Turn Based, or Variable Length Interoperative Competitive Chronology (still aroused). Eventually, rather than have the computer count time for you, someone just let the computer move the pieces and Dune II became the seed from which so much has come. Now, arguably, while this was going on, there was another genre of turn based strategy board game which made a similar real-time transition. Dungeons and dragons, as a board game, and not as a rich cooperative story telling experience with about as much potential as the people who played it (show of hands, who takes that to be a humanist statement of inspiration, and who takes that to be a snide backhanded insult–I’m just curious), was in this category, and was developed into games like WizWar and (much much much later) Hero’s quest or Talisman. Zelda is probably the best example of taking those board games into the realm of real time, with the oddly stunted throwbacks like Wasteland, Ultima, Bard’s Tale, and the goddamned seemingly endless Might and Magic series, which eventually ate itself by becoming Heroes of Might and Magic, the de rigeur mindless tabletop game throwback (now with more dice!).

None of these genres or games have action buttons. They do not represent the inherent power of the medium whatsoever. They’re fun, and having a computer track the variables in a board game makes for a far more interesting and less frustrating experience, but the Ghz chip was not built to run chess on the microsoft gaming zone.

The most well understood pedigree of the RTS genre, is not the actual source, but, instead, Dune II, which, in a way, was real time simply to conceal its lack of strategic depth. Range and damage were the currency in many games, but usually their terrain created situations which made the decisions about coming into range and doing damage more interesting. In Dune II there were 3 kinds of terrain, one impassible, one vanilla, and one with an arbitrary and very minor level of “danger.” The whole thing was strictly economy-limited, and there was little reason to produce anything but a horde of tanks. It would have been nice if every subsequent entrant into the genre had somehow realized that what we don’t need is artificial complexity about which raft of identical units we’re going to send en-masse at some challenge (THIS MAY BE IMPORTANT LATER).

I liked Overlord 1 enough to buy Overlord 2. I should say that here, just in case. But, keep in mind, I have a hedonistic, post-imperial Roman attitude about my game purchases. I have the collector’s edition of Frontline Fuel of War. I use the box to store change. I’ve never installed the game, let alone played it. At the time, I had it in my head, I think, that I wanted to support a studio that was parlaying a mod into a retail product (but it might also have been a subversive need to get everyone away from the Battlefield series — this was before Call of Duty 4 Changed Everything, Everywhere, Forever (if you’re rolling your eyes, we can hang out later)). I’ve sent Secret Technology a not inconsiderable amount of money (You can start with ‘I Make This. You Play This. We Are Enemies‘–and, while you’re free to form your own opinion about art, if you don’t like it, your opinion is wrong, and you’re an abscess). So believe me, when I say just because something bores me, or I hate it, or love it, or whatever, doesn’t mean I won’t/will buy it. The specific reason I’m buying Overlord 2 is that I was hoping it would be different (this, along with my enduring faith in the beauty of the human spirit rank among my dumbest thoughts).

If you don’t read the linked Overlord 1 article (you probably shouldn’t), I’ll just repeat the salient points here. There are only three adaptations to RTS on console I’ve seen work “successfully,” and by that, I mean, preserved the problems inherent to the RTS genre (throwing a mass of indistinguishable shit at another mass of same, and waiting for the dust to clear to see what happens + the irrelevance of environment — often by popular demand (BALANCE!)) as established (though not to the ideas and history behind it)* without introducing any new, novel problems which made it worse.

One is Goblin Commander, where your central avatar is where most of your intelligent decisions get made, and the ancillary creatures are controlled very simply, if at all. Demigod, Defense of the Ancients, and Dawn of War II have all approached the genre this way, though, hilariously, none of them are on console (yet, I suppose), and they’re all failures in the realm of control one way or another, in the best case (DoW II) just squandering the detail they worked into the units, and in the worst case (DemiGod) just being a way to iceskate through a system with no feedback whatsoever, loosely (through a glass, darkly), while the detail of the world actively works to make it harder to understand what you’re looking at and pick the parts you want to control.

Another is Pikmin. In this version, your “minions” are ammunition for your avatar, which is a simple construct and meant to be a focal point, only. In Pikmin’s case, the metaphor is so strong, you literally fling your minions at things you want them to deal with. This elegantly handles interface issues, since it extends the idea of a weapon or power that any number of console gamers are already familiar with, and doesn’t rely on the console metaphor, in the sense of a console that a NASA operator mans to identify and manage stirring of the O2 tanks, CO2 levels, and the number of xenomorphs active in the ductwork, rather than the joystick metaphor, which is what gaming consoles use (I have no idea how we got here from there, but who am I to piss into the wind . . . more than twice a week).

The third is Syndicate wars, which keeps unit numbers low, and uses them as divisible parts of an imaginary whole “squad” entity that you control directly, rather than individual units being grouped together temporarily. This holds with conventions like the Gradius Options or the deployable turrets from Perfect Darkness, a direct story sequel to Goldeneye 007 on the Playstation (which was weird, because Goldeneye came out on the Jaguar originally), which portrayed a 70s style vamp “Cate Archer” who was on a mission to defeat an ancient demonic Knight raised by the Third Reich (something wrong?). There were several sequences in that game, where the turret’s eyes-in-the-back-of-your-head quality was very useful. The basic idea of an automatic weapon system that takes some burden off you while you focus on an objective is as widespread as the homing missiles in Raiden or, well . . . homing missiles really anywhere.

The point is, in a game where you feel like a participant instead of a voyeur, it’s ok to have these helper objects. It’s even ok for them to be important. The three games above work alright. I’m not a huge Pikmin fan, and Goblin Commander has limited multiplayer replayability because the generals are simplified in order to accommodate the minion control scheme without needing to ever select a unit.

Overlord 2 makes you a passive participant in your own vaginization (join the uterination (man . . . conservative pundits have all the fun)). You start out playing what you think is a playful romp where you’ll overcome challenges with your minions and establish evil throughout the land, but, instead, you find yourself decorating your cave, stomping cute things because they’re cute (which, in the abstract, if you think about it, means you’re basically addicted to cute things — the part where you butcher them is really just a Lenny-like reflex), and accumulating a social circle of girlfriends, as my grandmother used the term, and not as the Notorious B.I.G. would have. You end up going out to commit evil because it’s your 9 to 5 that gives you the disposable evil-income you need to play out your perverted nesting instinct (in the Abyss!). Ultimately, you’ll hit bottom when you spend minion energy to have a minion dressed as a grim-reaper (so pwecious!) bring minions back from the dead — and, upon jumping out of your mini-river Styx (oh my goodness, that’s so clever! Sheryl! SHERYL! Did you see what they did here? They have their own river crossing for lost souls in the basement! Isn’t that darling? I’m going to talk to Cragnord about doing that for us. We’re planning on redoing our cavern), the minion, dressed for all the world like Paris Hilton’s meth addicted chihuahua on Halloween, will actually run to the death-minion and hug him. Why not just have plush sea turtles carebear staring my little ponies with the little mermaid in . . . excuse me, my mons pubis itches.

Overlord 2, in short, is the most clever skinning of Animal Crossing we’re ever likely to see.

Now, alot of people think Animail (see what I did there? — for those not familiar, you get alot of mail in that game) Crossing is great, because it’s a friendly environment where you can safely pursue self-expression with a variety of fun and easy minigames set in a community filled with anthropomorphic NIGHTMARE CREATURES. It is not friendly. Or an environment. There’s nothing safe about it. A fantastic writer for this very site declared Free Realms to be a vile satanic plot to distort the minds of children. That’s all true, but the only reason SOE could even coherently articulate the idea for husking young minds is because Nintendo (already responsible for Pokemon, the non-game you share with your friends) embraced and distributed Animal Crossing on the DS, trapping otherwise productive human beings in an environment that I could describe as Sims-like, only without any sex, crime, or death, in other words, without the fun part.

When Link (<– this is a character from a popular video game) cuts the grass, he’s doing it because it hides treasure, because it conceals secrets, and above-all, because he has to walk somewhere, at least before he gets Epona (<– this is a character’s horse from a popular video game) and . . . well . . . why not (<– this is a character motivation from a popular video game). Nobody asks him to cut it, and the fact that it grows back instantly is, more than anything, there to let you know that you’re not playing Zelda to cut the fucking grass. But wait, you can do other things! Fish!

Fishing is stupid. I don’t mean fishing in video games for imaginary fish that you can’t really eat is stupid, I mean Fishing, in Real Life is stupid. It is the laziest way to hunt that mankind has ever devised. I’m not talking about Alaska’s most dangerous Crabs or pursuing an angry mammalicurian Orca pod with whatever-the-pacific-northwest-native-american’s-used-before-they-had-compressed-air-stud-shooter-guns. I’m not saying spear fishing is stupid, or the crazy shit pacific islanders do, where they dive in and basically grab the fish and knife it like it’s an aquatic Brooklyn. I’m saying the *only* reason people get a case of beer, get on a boat, put a worm on a hook, and then wait for hours is because they A) hate their wives B) can’t drink in the house and C) are worried that any other combination of tools, sports, or hobbies will end up with personal injury due to their plan re: B. I’ve fished. Sober. I suppose if I were starving it would beat a poke in the eye with a sharp stick. In other words, Fishing is a manly activity in the same sense that running off with your tail tucked between your legs to hide your misery from the world in a bottle is a manly activity, because girls tend to get together with friends and eat icecream instead. The inclusion of Fishing in everything digital is a designer’s middle finger to his own medium. “You’re worried what you’re doing is antisocial and stupid? What if we make you fish, dumbshit? How entertained do you feel now?”

Cut the grass, bitch.

I promised that sending a mass of units at another mass of units would somehow become relevant later. In Overlord 2, you frequently send a mass of units at whatever you need to send them at. Sometimes the color matters. Sometimes you have to call them back and then send them out again. The most entertaining permutation of this mechanic is when you “sweep” them, via joystique through waypoints, in order, occasionally more than once to complete the objective, which, if I’m not being clear enough, is to decorate your house with obelisk shaped kitsch.

Visually, Overlord 2 is one of the finest examples of its kind — the free-time roach trap, where you walk in, and get stuck to the ground by cuteness and pretty environments and ok-sometimes humor. I was down in the basement of my abyssal penthouse, looking at the rooms I had to fill with potpourri, and thought aloud (to no one in particular), “Wow, this is incredibly beautiful. I need to hang out down here more.” Then, as the thought finished, and the last syllable reverberated in my own ears, the part of my brain that had been asleep (or whatever happens when you get task-focused) awoke outraged, and the game went sailing through an open window.

Overlord 2 made me hate Overlord 1, and for that valuable perspective, it gets half a star.
