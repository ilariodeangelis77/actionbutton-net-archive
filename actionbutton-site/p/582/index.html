Prototype (**1/2)

a review of
prototype
a videogame by radical entertainment
published by activision
for the playstation 3, xbox 360, and windows
text by samuel kite
score: 1/2 (out of four)

Bottom line: Prototype is “the sheer joy of eating.”

1/2

People throw around the term “overpowered” because they’ve been abused for so long they don’t know what fun means. Prototype is the sort of game that abuses them.

Codependence is fine if people are cooperating. Symbiosis isn’t evil, and the emotions that force people to behave cooperatively don’t care about dignity. They’re only worried about results. If it can make a Man o’ War jellyfish, then it’s good enough to make a twelve-year-old whoop like he’s seeing the new G.I. Joe movie, and unless we’ve suffered brain damage, we’ve all got that twelve-year-old inside us, ready to woop a little, even while we roll our eyes. However, abusive codependence is a walrus of a different musk. Most of us have been abused by games. So severely abused, that the question of their mechanics is so far beyond the realm of what you can offer comment upon, that it’s like trying to refute some creationist statement along the lines of, “Well, if the earth is so old, then how come scientists haven’t found the missing link?”. You’ve got serious Dwarf-Fortress work to do in order to create the kind of environment where you could begin to disagree with the underlying premise. Prototype is one of the few games where you can and should think about the basic mechanics as something different than the execution or overall pacing and design.

The game is about balancing dynamic tensions. On the one hand, you’ve got these methods and powers that allow you to go apeshit and destroy everything nearby, and leap several blocks away to relative safety. On the other hand, you have these very specific, small-seeming attacks you need to use to destroy things that are just as squirrely as you, and want to chase you.

There’s a version of events where you want to think “falling out of the sky like a meteor, crushing sixty square feet of pavement, and destroying a car before grabbing a victim I need to devour for the Web of Intrigue (TM) has caused enough chaos that I accidentally killed that victim and cannot eat him” means that the game has bad controls and is asking you to do annoying tasks. There’s another version of events where you do the same thing, but think of it like trying to eat the ghosts in the right order as Pacman, or defeat the Space Invaders without destroying your own shield, or rescue the humans in Defender. It’s the delicacy that makes a superhuman game interesting. If Hulk was boring, it was because you had little recourse but to be hulkier and hulkier against a legion of assholes who would never stop bothering you. Crackdown and GTA both trade on your limitations to sustain interest. You never get an invincible car. You’re never strong enough to destroy a building. Trees laugh at you and continue standing, unscathed.

In Prototype, you can destroy (some specific) buildings, and toss tanks and cars aside casually as you run around like a crazy person. You can make a crater whenever you please. The fun part is *not* making a crater, or, conversely, all the incidental craters that happen while you’re trying to focus on a specific adversary.

If Crackdown is Mario, where the sheer joy of jumping is expressed in tasks involving more jumping (so that you can jump even higher, and do more things while in the air, such as have cucumber sandwiches and hold a high tea), then Prototype is Pacman, where the sheer joy of eating is expressed in tasks involving more eating, but, more specifically, the delicate balance of deciding *when* to eat what. In Crackdown, you’re virtually never required to avoid jumping (or driving — though the assertion from some dev interviews that it was somehow a driving game with an action game in it, rather than an action game with the same amount of driving every other GTA had, always confused me). Self-control is a mostly meaningless concept. There’s really nothing in the world of Crackdown that deserves to live. This is the hooker-syndrome from Grand Theft Auto. Why not be utterly misanthropic and kill the hooker whose services you just retained (”fucked” is the post-modernist term) and get your money back? It’s not as if she means something. She didn’t even exist until you turned onto that dockside street and honked your horn. What difference does it make how long you keep hitting her with a bat. I mean . . . Uh. Hey, guy. You . . . you done, or . . . ? How long you going to keep doing that?

Prototype manages to bring a puppy-like joy to entertainment by truly engcouraging you to do anything and everything. If you die, the game asks if you want to go into free roam mode. It knows you might be pissed. It wants you to have fun. When you fail a mission, you can start it again, or you can go to another one nearby. When you accidentally destroy the Web of Intrigue target, there’s no reason not to utterly mutilate a city block in a ridiculous tantrum — that’s what it’s there for. If you’re ready to continue with the story, then go, continue. The things you shouldn’t destroy are like biscuits your master says you can’t eat, and if you can just Sit. Good boy. Then you can have the biscuit! It’s amazing. Eventually, you’ll be taught to roll over and lie down, and all kinds of marvelous things. This is what games do to us anyway, but to have it done to you in a way that feels like the love you have for a dog seems nice.

That’s the kind of codependence that really hits the spot. I’d rather be adorable than some vexing ven diagram of “Hard Core” slash “Casual” that makes developers miserable no matter what they do, even while their efforts are sabotaged by business degrees.

On the other hand, the Potato people own this world, and that’s off-putting.

Then there’s this logical fallacy called “begging the question,” which a certain segment of the know-it-all species likes to put under the same heading as Alanis Morisette’s “Ironic,” a song so deeply ironic, it was using the word “ironic” to mean something other than its literal intention. The way it goes, usually, is that someone says, “It begs the question . . . are we human? or are we dancers?” (The answer, I think, is, “If Hunter S. Thompson ever saw a cross between Fall Out Boy and Coldplay; Killers concert, he’d probably rush the stage and beat the lead singer to death with a plank of splintered wood — and I don’t even think Hunter S. Thompson is that cool . . . ” dilettantes), and then someone else says, “Begging the question means you say something having yet to be proven as though it were fact, asshole,” and then stand with arms folded athwart a sense of accomplishment (represented by a walrus).

But, in reality, this brain thing is always pruning behaviors for efficiency (since it is lazy), and that leads to ingrained reactions. So when you are discussing something that is, for whatever reason, wrong, and you want to express its wrongness while also saying that people don’t realize how wrong it is, and now I’m going to identify for you where we went wrong, all in one tight little phrase, you can say “It begs the question: THIS IS THE QUESTION WE SHOULD BE ASKING RIGHT HERE.”

So, Prototype’s gameplay begs the question (by which I mean, as a complete work of western art, it purports to make a statement, or, in the post-modernist view, is a statement regardless of whether it has an intention or not, and that statement is relying on facts as proven which have yet to be such and also walruses), “Is there a difference between abusing a player and providing a challenge?”

I’m now going to assert that, yes, there is, and I’m going to beg the question at several points without calling either the fact that I’m doing it or what question I’m begging out as I go (as I’ve done above), and you, the ABDN fan, or tragically lost DHS Internet spy, can decide which approach is easier to understand.

There’s a review on this site mentioning an old japanese game which seemed like a cross between Dark Tower and Gauntlet (retroactive edit: Tower of Druaga; it’s mentioned in the Super Mario Brothers 3 review and is not, as I thought, reviewed unto itself — it just seems like it because the SMB3 review is the Rocket Jockey (**** if I get around to it) of blog posts). It is used as an example of the social landscape that could evolve in gamer culture when you make extremely obscure puzzles, and also how it doesn’t exist anymore. Also, how obscurity as a premise is interesting when it’s released in 1984 (the significance of the date is no coincidence, by which I mean it is entirely coincidence). The series of thoughts laid out in the above Druagaien example work something like this; visual language is powerful. It’s powerful almost precisely because it transcends spoken language. It can be used as a tool to express, quickly, and efficiently, for example, directions, or motivation. Since video games, in the moment, have an attention span that is nearly nonexistent, visual language is used to teach you and orient you quickly. However, video games — by their enjoyable nature — are also long love affairs, and like all art, have an essential social component shared by everyone who consumes the art. So, you can sacrifice the visual language in order to force a translative reflex in the sick human mind to occur, and thereby automatically bestow your game with a rich narrative which, simultaneously, generates an accompanying culture almost by accident.

In a vacuum of information, the human instinct is to catalogue and diagnose. This works great when bark is missing from a tree, and it turns out there’s a bear somewhere nearby. In a vacuum of purpose, the human instinct seems to be, by and large, to first deny it, then become angry about it, then depressed, then bargain with the nothingness, and finally to accept the purposelessness and shut down. For this reason, the invention and cultivation of purpose is one of our prime pursuits, both in our deepest reflexes (go forth and multiply), and in our cognitive awareness (mirror in the bathroom). The proposition that a lack of information creates a culture is something we take for granted. Nobody disputes that not knowing what, exactly, the “clone wars” were in the original Star Wars was better than watching two cartoons about them (oh . . . it’s a war where clones fought robots). Statements laden with possibility allow our imaginations, which are infinitely better at creating a wet dream than reality (or another person’s imagination) is, for any given effort on an artist’s part (I’m assuming intelligent design of wet dreamers, but only because I think a nocturnal emission is too perfectly designed to force you to wash your sheets on a regular basis to be an accident — none of this statement means what it sounds like, btw) to fill in the gaps masterfully, or, more importantly, leave gaps unfilled, with only a sketch-like outline of feelings and sensations to preserve a perfect contradiction. Environments laden with possibility offer the same proposition to our physical and mental activities, though, and this implies what? A bottomless pit in which to pour our blood. We cannot do everything. We can only dream it. Play time must have a purpose, or boredom will save us from the “freedom,” to play with dreams that conserve our vitality. We’re all swimming until we die.

We are marginally more complex than a sperm.

In Super Mario Bros., you move to the right until you run out of rights to run to. In Zelda, you are shown a cave, and must enter it to discover that your purpose is to find and enter caves. In Pac-Man, dots disappear as you move over them, and there is a finite board, so, one assumes, you must finish eating all the dots. However, there is no number of dots for you to eat which will end the game. Yes, there is a practical limit, because in the old days, the hijinks computers got up to were hilarious, but there is no intentional end. Pac-Man is making it clear from the outset, like Cookie Monster, you will eat and eat, and the uneaten crumbs will fall out of the black piece of felt which is your mouth; you cannot fill that void, and you will never stop hungering. The point of Pac-Man is in the points. By putting a score up there, a purpose is suggested. Is the game any different with this purpose? Couldn’t you have played Pac-Man to eat as many cherries as possible? Couldn’t you have played it to avoid eating ghosts, or to only eat particular colors of ghosts? Couldn’t you have played Pac-Man for a survival-time record, avoiding eating all the dots or super dots?

My father, who was born before video games, and also has the rare and bizarre ability to define his own purpose, once suggested I try to play Asteroids as a “Peacenik.” That is, avoid hurting any asteroids or U.F.O.s for as long as possible without dying. At the time, I tried it for a while, and concluded that, while this was a novel way to appreciate the game, ultimately, white dots come out the front of the triangle when the space bar is pressed, and that, as I’m sure you agree, is a formidable proposition. He is not into scoring points, as a rule, because (to be reductive) he used to shoot skeet at a competitive level, and, ultimately, that activity seemed to have an unstated purpose of improving the number of delicious geese you could eat if you had ammunition and were hungry. Asteroid management in deep-space, tragically, is not yet on Monster.com’s hot jobs list.

Years later, it turns out he’s some kind of fucking oracle, because there was an Achievement in Geometry Wars to go for a certain number of seconds without killing anything, and not dying. It’s literally the same triangle.

Two great advances in the statement of purpose took place in my lifetime. One was the gated reward, as seen in the level up concept from RPGs, and the other was the unlockable mystery (also known as the “rare drop”). In the one instance, making the score mechanism ouroboran, and in the other, harnessing the human desire to make information relevant, in order to manufacture purpose. These means of stating purpose had, previously, been used by religion to spread, so I should say the use, in video games, took place in my lifetime. Metal Slug Jesus road into Bandaithlehem on a donkey (which had huge machine guns on it — a seriously sweet piece of ass). These are Interactive Design’s best practices in the medium of simulated playspaces. The strength of the game is that it allows us to learn and harness long-term strategic thinking in the accomplishment of complex tasks with an extremely low barrier to entry, and high retention rate. Whereas someone might get bored trying to learn how to tune a race car to run faster in real life, as it is laborious, and expensive, and prone to a sinful lifestyle of excess, tuning a car in the sufficiently carefully simulated Gran Turismo not only is an entertaining end in itself which might snare any number of spurting sprouts, but is genuinely relevant back in the real world (where, if you die, you also die in the matrix). The problem is one of excessive focus.

What is the point of Final Fantasy? It is to defeat the weird thing that you’ve been sort of (but not really clearly) told needs to be defeated. Isn’t it also to win the card game? What about a championship Blitzball team? What about golden chocobos? What about playing the piano? What about just hanging out and getting to know the N.P.C. who says, “. . .”? In Crystal Chronicles, you are filling a chalice, but wouldn’t it be nice to explore alternative sources of energy which might protect your village from the omnipresent threat of the Purple? Final Fantasy has a tendency to offer you something very specific to accomplish, which is to wring every last vaguely expositional piece of entertainment from its soaking substance. But those worlds are huge. There’s much, much more in them, even by accident. While, without purpose, we would be largely lost, with purpose we have target fixation. Unless stopping to smell the roses were a goal with “points” in it, how would we know to do so?

Achievements to the rescue. What, at first, seemed like a crude and evil way to manipulate us, has become a way to reconsider the possibilities of a playspace in a way which is seemless, and avoids competing with the game’s own mechanisms to encourage your participation. Tragically, for the most part, these achievements are used only to reinforce the primary purpose of the game, but in Grand Theft Auto, Crackdown, and Saint’s Row, the side-track purposes are finding great ways to encourage a broader experience of the given game-worlds, and, fundamentally, the mechanical tools you’re given to experience them. There are, in fact, plenty of games in which bizarre behavior which might have occurred to you on your own (and might not) is given an opportunity to experience wider circulation among thinking participants.

But, they can be poorly implemented, as said, and, in fact, you can do such a shitty job with your interface that your entire game suffers and is nearly a pile of shit as a result.

Prototype’s basic mechanics are extremely impressive. No “weapon,” when upgraded, becomes a key to open an enemy shaped door — they are merely different styles of play, informed more by immediate circumstances, than by arbitrary bullshit rock-paper-scissors mechanics. The movement, and maneuver, while smooth and auto-piloted to a great degree for you, are also very touchy, and challenging to master. Whereas Assassin’s Creed could feel like running a riding lawnmower at full throttle, in terms of the challenge to steering it, Prototype feels much more like trying to balance a Suzuki M109R through a tricky uphill turn on road half covered in gravel — if you don’t hit any gravel, sure, it’s pretty easy — but that’s a big “if.” The character is an overpowered sports car in a demanding race track. The trick is not to overdo it, rather than to always make the best use of his abilities.

The game makes some effort to clarify this purpose. There are people to eat who will reveal tiny fragments of the story to you through interesting and well done montages (all of them presented in a twisted nightmare fashion of absorbing someone’s memories). There are side-missions to learn how to kill quickly, or to support one side over another in a given area. There are (of particular interest) footrace challenges which are much more challenging than Crackdown’s for the simple reason that your character has too much throttle. Zeus will land on that rooftop, and then scoot half a block away if you aren’t careful, rather than turning left, and traveling toward the next way-point. Never have I played a super-hero game or platformer which made racing a person feel as deep and nuanced as racing a vehicle. Without the excuse to get a gold medal in these footrace challenges, I don’t know that I would have been encouraged, sufficiently, to learn how to use Zeus’s movement to be so precise and fast at the same time — because it’s not the sort of game where you have to be precise and fast, unless you’re doing these footraces.

If you realize that Prototype is a game with incredible basic mechanics that shine when you’re put in specific situations requiring you to use them well, then you will see an absolutely brilliant game — probably one of the best “brawler” games made in recent years. Certainly more complicated than Devil May Cry, which, despite having fighting game-like sensibilities in the dodging and weaving, and encouraging you to mix up your attacks, never uses mobility and terrain to change the fundamentals of your challenges; it is, in general, a series of arena matches. Prototype is an MMA fight that takes place in an avalanche on Mars (they have really large mountains there) — it’s just interesting to manipulate the combat mechanics and the movement mechanics at the same time.

But the UI design for this game sets it far beneath DMC or Ninja Gaiden (let alone Shinobi).

When you start the game, you are run through a short tutorial in which your character sprouts each weapon and you’re instructed to use it for a few moments to feel what it’s like. This happens in the middle of a chaotic warzone. Unfortunately, you cannot discover the use of the weapons yourself — you are locked into the one the game is “showing” you. This forces you to not learn the best mechanic the game has; switching weapons and mutations on the fly in order to change your approach to the chaos around you. As you end this tutorial segment, you’re told to chase down and devour some specific soldier who has important information you need. This introduces you to the story-telling method of the game, and the Pac-Man mechanic. However, it does it by showing you something which makes no sense, and then pulls a time reversal “last is first” (was clever the first time, but is no longer clever) narration trick where you find yourself in an alley-way moments after having “escaped” without any memory of who you are. As you jump a fence (unable to jump as high as you could before), and run down the street to avoid helicopters and enemy soldiers, you find you have none of the abilities and toys you had before, and must build them back up. This comes back to the abuse issue I touched on to begin with.

In some games, your gun does 2 damage on stage one and 20 damage on stage 10, because the enemies have ten times as many hit-points on stage 10, and the game is trying to express to you that you are “improving.” However, this is an abusive bullshit trope that accomplishes nothing. In some other games, you start with golden flaming blades and are immediately robbed of them in order to make your vendetta line up with the character’s vendetta, as determined beforehand in the script. This is by definition abusive, but at least it has something to do with the narrative. But, more and more often, games simply lock away parts of the mechanics until later to make sure that you sample all of their pieces, one at a time, and not because the pieces can’t function together. While it’s totally okay for you to start Mechwarrior 2 in a light mech, because the missions are for light (and fast) mechs, and then, only later, to get in assault mechs (because the later missions are for assault mechs), it’s not okay for you to start without a magic rod that can make a block on the floor, and then receive that magic rod moments before entering a room in which there is a button on the floor, but no block to drag (we’re talking to you, Super Mario Galaxy, although it might not seem like it, because we were covertly referencing another Nintendo franchise). At least, though, you could argue that by giving you the tool, then showing you how to use the tool, then asking you to use the tool, you learn how to use the tool (you tool, you).

However, when you cannot execute on the learning portion correctly, you’re fucking up the hardwood floor. Prototype, the game, which takes place on the Prototype playground, requires you to change mutations to accomplish what you need to accomplish, and to do so while managing the main character, who, again, like a Suzuki M109R, has more than enough power to run himself off the road. In the introduction sequence, by locking you up, and not showing you the need for delicacy, they are essentially teaching you how not to play the game. Later, when you find yourself irritated by the foot races or bizarre missions inside bases, the only reason you could be irritated is because nobody told you what game you were going to be playing.

The cinematics were obviously shoehorned in after some pustulant sack of shit who was in charge of ruining the UI declared that the vision-quest memory absorption sequences were too “strange” and “hard to follow,” and, instead, someone would need to cut holes in the faces of the models built for the game-sequences and use them in cinematics. A couple pissed-off animators probably rented a motion capture studio for a weekend, and halfassed some sequences involving the completely worthless script that the pissed-off writer had to put down at the last minute to accomodate this unplanned-for series of barely-expositional sequences. At the end, instead of Burnout Paradise with people in it, you are treated to a vast open world with a Star waypoint which inadvertently indicates to you that you should drag your ass through the story, since that’s the core of the game, when the story is the last fucking thing this game has to offer anyone. Don’t get me wrong: the story, if you were to completely excise the parts with cinematics and only take it on the basis of the “main” interludes at special chapter points (which aren’t executed as poorly, and sometimes don’t contain potato people), sticking just to the interesting and strange parts where you devour someone’s memories and try to piece together a narrative from fragments involving sights, sounds, and dialogue that don’t necessarily match up (this is actually very good from the standpoint of the culture previously discussed), is a valuable tool in teaching you that the game is Pac-Man, and that people are cherries, or, occasionally, super-dots.

Prototype is clearly a game that was originally meant to be a challenging action game that took the concept of super-powered main characters out of the ego-trip where you simply explode in ever greater fireballs the entire universe, like a Final Fantasy character in a platformer, and into the realm of the thoroughbred racer. A realm where the power is a tool that must be used carefully, because there’s so much of it, that there’s too much of it. Restraint is what we play video games for, in a sense. We restrain ourselves from making the wrong choices. We take pleasure in our sense of timing. We enjoy learning and growing and doing and, above all, not doing (the same thing over and over, due to failure).

Then someone did this tutorial bullshit, locking away ninety-percent of the powers in a haphazard fashion divorced from any intention of design. Then someone worried that there wasn’t enough “story” (because story wasn’t the point — the game was the point), and created a false expectation with waypoints. Then someone made sure that every second that you tried to get something accomplished, the game would freeze and notify you that the power you’re using works in XYZ way (usually poorly written so as to be confusing), and disrupting your rhythm and control. Then someone thought it’d be an awesome idea to add in powers which allowed you to do more damage, be invulnerable at the moment of death, and destroy everything in a one-block radius without consequences. Then someone put in a leveling system.

Here’s the quintessential truth of this games greatness. There are these explorer tokens you can get, sure. They’re just worth points — whatever. Explore or don’t. But then there are these “hint” tokens. They contain tutorial information. They are superior to actual tutorials, though, because:

A) You get them when you get them, and not when the game decides you have to be told something, making them a response to a desire instead of an interruption.

B) A marvelous mystery hinting at the depths of the game (and the game does have depths, though the lexical presentation is pretty half-assed, in keeping with the theme of horrible UI design).

C) They seem to have come from a time when the game was going to drop your bizarre anti-hero into the city and then watch you blossom like a hideous flower, discovering your powers as you pressed buttons, and not, as it has later become, through an arbitrary leveling system.

These hint tokens are amazing. They seem to suggest a game where Zeus, a disease that thinks he’s a man (oh shit, potato spoiler, I guess) wakes up in a morgue, escapes, and then begins to try to understand what he is and what has happened to him, while mastering abilities he doesn’t understand. In this sense, mastery does not mean “utilize to their full effect,” because that part is easy. Master should mean “learn to control.”

If he is a man, then the people he kills are something that should weigh on his conscience. I see vestiges of a time when those people weren’t supposed to die, in an “after action” report which summarizes damage done to zombies, unjust military presence, and the people. You can disguise yourself and walk among them — you can cooperate with said military presence to defeat the monstrous menaces in midtown.

I’m convinced that there was a time during its development when Prototype was one of the best games ever made. It has since been rendered into paste to make it similar to every other bullshit thing where some dude climbs walls and kills people with super powers.

–Action Button Dot Net
