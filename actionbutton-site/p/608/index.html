Shadow Complex (***)


a review of shadow complex
a videogame by chair
published by microsoft game studios
text by samuel kite
score: (out of four)

Bottom line: Shadow Complex is “Controid.”


This is not a review of Rocket Jockey — or, at least, it’s not going to start out as one, unless it starts to get really wordy. So about Shadow Complex . . .

Defense of the Ancients is a Warcraft III map which takes the RPG principles and micromanagement of the hero units in vanilla Warcraft III, and combines it with the tragically amazing tower-defense game type which also seems to have reached genre parity with real boys seemingly as a direct result of its existence as a campaign map in Warcraft III.  There could be a footnote here about the power of the Blizzard map editors to spawn game extensions, to say nothing of their influence on the direction of genres, as a whole, but it would mean even more paragraphs. Eschewing that, recently, there’s been a spontaneous developer movement to bring DotA to the general gaming public in the form of a studio-polished piece, rather than the insular and permanently community-harrassed permutations of the original map. One of them is Heroes of Newerth, and the other is League of Legends.

I have never, and probably will never, play the original DotA. The first taste I had of this game was the HoN beta (NDA recently loosened), which, to an external observer, appears to be a faithful reproduction and incremental improvement  of the DotA experience, as recorded by Basshunter. LoL, is also extremely similar to this alleged original experience which I have reconstructed anthropologically without actually playing it, myself (from primary sources, which makes it practically brazen truth as misreported by the people with the most to gain by distorting it), but seems to be making additional tweaks which change the experience somewhat. To summarize the divergent points of view, S2 Games, the developer behind HoN, believe that DotA is great, but deeply limited by the Warcraft III engine, and deserves its own life as a game — not just as an extension of WC3 multiplayer. As a consequence, they’ve done everything in their power to reproduce the practical experience of the engine exactly, including the oddball limitations and quirks which became inseparable from the game design. Riot’s position, on the other hand, is that WC3’s graphics are dated, and need some refurbishment, but that the underpinnings of DotA are rock solid, and need to be faithfully brought to a wider audience. As a consequence of that belief, they’ve made a game which, graphically, appears to be roughly similar to WC3, though possibly a little more cartoony, and have made important, and rather significant changes to the game mechanics in order to make it more playable and less user unfriendly. If these positions do not jive with your expectations, for instance, because you speak english, and know what words mean, that’s only because you haven’t experienced the DotA metagame.

All games, to a greater or lesser extent, hinge on the mechanism of perseverance being more important than skill or, really, any other advantage. There’s great reading to be had on this subject in the broad arena of life from The Drunkard’s Walk, or Keegan’s The Face of Battle. The long and the short of it, is, the baseball fan’s fascination with statistics and strategy can be seen as a microcosm of the general human inability to comprehend the large number of variables at work in life, in general (whether in the sense that those statistics which are meaningful in a team’s success are often misread, or misweighted, to the point that there are still, on a regular basis new sets of statistics which have a huge impact on how players are drafted, etc. — or in the sense that, on a given day when someone is up to bat, the roll of stats a dedicated fan can discuss about the player does little to predict how they do at that moment). Our instincts and racial memories come from statistical averages, and are generally more capable than our intellect in determining effective behavior. The intellect, really, is only a tool of comprehension, not of determination: it accurately identifies that which we fight or flee from, not which of those polar opposite decisions is the better to actually make. At our core, what drives us to be social, cooperative, and even civilized are fundamental and primitive urges that predate our existence as modern humans. A dog is as sophisticated. But a cat is not (what is *wrong* with you people?)

DotA, however, takes this principle and distills it into a man’s man kind of level, where the game itself isn’t even the point. While there certainly is a great deal of specific information to remember, a large variety of heroes and cooperative strategies, especially against other heroes, and even room for someone to develop skill in maneuvering their hero, like any other fighting game, at the end of the day, the DotA game is too random an environment to produce what amounts to long-term effective strategies in the same way that you might be able to in a 1 vs. 1 fighting game, or an FPS, where the capabilities of each unit are much more similar, even between each other. For example, if you’re playing Team fortress 2, you never need to wonder if a spy has taken his disguise-power yet, or if his stab-you-in-the-back power is on cooldown. As a consequence, it’s possible to learn effective behaviors that work within the game, and are not merely a matter of wandering around at random (read: determinedly attempting to play well using skill and cooperation (don’t whine)), hoping for the best.

Obviously, it’s never the case the purely random behavior is as good as behavior with a purpose, but as variables accumulate, the most important behaviors are the most generically useful. In the case of life, survival and reproduction are paramount, and no amount of charcoal on a cave-wall is going to make running away from a bear any worse of an idea. It doesn’t matter if you’re Davey Crockett: doing something dangerous is always a bad risk, and the womb-tenders are not attracted to risk takers — they’re attracted to persevering survivors who take valid risks for useful payouts (for instance, ensuring the survival of a mate). DotA, then, comes down to these kinds of decisions — a tension between primitive urges to flee or fight, and the risk that you’ll pick the wrong approach this time. So, ultimately, a given round of DotA is decided by the people who have better conservation of morale, or, if you prefer, the courage to keep going, even when the chips are down: to continue to try to make the best decisions possible in the worst circumstances, and, ultimately, outlast their opponents.

In the aftermath of these contests, people will be given to discuss, like a baseball enthusiast, the specifics of the match that produced the outcome, hoping that, by analyzing the shit out of the events in the game, they will gain a measure of control over it. This does not produce a real result, in the sense that they gain no extra control, and really learn nothing from the experience (sure, this time, it turned out a high level character with a specific item became a late-game lynch pin of victory, but only because the person playing it experienced an extremely precise series of events that put them in that position, and then received cooperation from their team that made their performance notable — the final tally of statistics makes the data you pull from the experience utterly meaningless in terms of how it should affect your own behavior (you could argue, undergrad style, that all that knowledge just sinks in and has value, man, but like, if you want to succeed in the world and get a goat sodomizing business degree with “management experience” and a 401k, you’re going to want some cut and dried, bottom line, bullet points, and if you can’t have those, it’s just noise coming out a gaping hole in the ozone that is wasting your life like melanoma-inducing cosmic rays burning up your golden years)). These attempts at control are a byproduct of a genuinely useful ability to adapt to and manage specific variables, such as mood, which affect survival, as well as a cruel joke played on thinking creatures by an evil universe.

DotA captures this ennui very effectively by creating, in the truest sense of the word, a 2nd life in a primitive environment that represents the unfairness of it all, and then scores you on your performance. Of course, in real life, nobody ever really sinks your battleship to provide that final cathartic resolution. Death is the ultimate game-ending variable, at which point your score is immaterial and there’s nothing to learn from the experience, anymore. You are never trying to reincarnate and being kicked from zygotes because your win/loss stats suck.

I bring this up because of the concept of the ‘Metroidvania’ game, and its new cache in a world where subgenres are combinations of game titles that the quotey fingers Gaming Press  indulge themselves in inventing at random according to what the least intelligent among them notices first through the jaded glaze of their cynical heavy-lidded disdain for anything and everything good in the field, and puppy-like hyperactivity over sequels, spiritual sequels, prequels, or anything that can be prefaced with the phrase ‘from the designer of . . .’, seems to dominate the discussion of almost any platformer with a persistent map. Most platformers, now, have a persistent map of some kind, in the sense that you can return to earlier areas, or the same area is used for all levels of play. But so few games which aren’t actually Castlevania sequels have done much with the concepts introduced by Metroid or Castlevania 3.

Metroid did two things for the platform genre: it allowed you to shoot doors, and it put ammo everywhere. Another perspective on this would be that doors were locked, and keys were everywhere. But, in either case, the Metroid room was not about killing all the enemies, though you could certainly do that, or about finding all the pieces of equipment, though you could certainly do that. The adversary in Metroid was the very unknown, itself. The world, filled with strange hidden paths, and forcefields that yeilded only to super missiles, and not missiles, or only to bullets, and not wads of tapioca flung at high velocity out the ends of a socket wrench on a sport bike with legs, was more important than what was inside it. The difference between a Metroid, where the right amount of effort would yield five more rockets to play with, and Zelda, where the right amount of effort would get you rupees, or, only very occasionally, a new item, often with exactly one specific use (in the later, grimmer, darker, drearier years), or Contra, where the right amount of effort would give you access to the Laser shot — a vastly inferior option to the Spread shot, was in its completely ‘XP’-less way to encourage your growth in the gameworld. While Zelda (Metroid without gravity) tended to think in terms of keys to doors, and direct upgrades of the behaviors of single items (boomerang is great for doing X, magic boomerang is great for doing something exactly like X, only accomplishable with the magic boomerang), Metroid wanted you to find more missiles, and then *fire* more missiles. This is its distinction from other games where specific items and powers were unlocked by certain challenges — often a boss creature. Metroid, basically, loves you, and it wants you to believe that life is about the courage to explore, the accumulation of wisdom and experience that eventually lead you to a metaphor where you fire rocket after rocket into a giant exposed brain.

I’m reasonably certain that was sci-fi presentation of the hippy movement, where the evil brain represents your inability to feel the love in the world, man.

Maybe Zebes is a pitlike prison of your body, and the spaceship represents a pill with a payload of an armored vixen, who represents a mind-blowing chemical experience.

Castlevania, on the other hand, was always about getting to specific bosses and defeating them. It was the ‘classic’ we refer to when we discuss video games and mention the ‘classics’. Even Zelda, which was ridden with sub-bosses, and simple gimmicky rooms with grasping hands, or moving blocks was too much of a grab bag experience in comparison. In its early incarnations, before all the main characters became hopelessly pretty, the game did not force you to proceed to quite the same degree that most similar, boss-focused games had. While there were a number of experiences like platformers, and a number with bosses, it was this series which melded the shooter and platformer genres in a series of climactic confrontations.

It becomes very basic, really, Metroid was Zelda with gravity. Castlevania is 1942 (or Raiden, or R-Type, or Gradius, or whatever) with gravity. Metroid+vania, then, is Zelda as a shooter.

Zelda was not supposed to be a shooter, and, in the same way that I may or may not have previously bitched endlessly about the development of the turn-based strategy game genre into a fighting game via Starcraft, the ultimate expression of that being the Defense of the Ancients, which is more sportlike than game, for the precise reason that, on any given sunday, anything can happen (and someone on your team will call you retarded over Vent/Teamspeak/Skype because of it), I could bitch a little bit about how the strange road it traveled has diminished the ultimate fusion of these genres. But that’s not what this is about.

This is about two games that always knew what they were about, and, despite their distortions, have remained true to their premise. Super Mario brothers, and, basically, Contra. Games which are *only* about momentum.

In any given action subgenre, there seems to be a decision to be made between the single screen puzzle and the multi-screen puzzle. It gets somewhat more complicated to parse, but you could consider all multi-screen puzzles to be waves launched on a single screen in response to player actions. Something like Galaga and Space Invaders being the same game, only the seamlessness of the challenges makes the difference. So, in Zelda, when you go to one room, fight several monsters, get a key, and then backtrack through three or four rooms, fighting or dodging other monsters, to finally arrive at the room with a door which you had the key for — this might have all happened on a single screen, with enemies spawning after you defeated the previous ‘wave’, if only you didn’t mind not seeing the exact same interior scroll by several times (and to be fair, in later iterations of the series, the scenery is more interesting, though it usually exists only to needlessly prolong the originally intriguing game mechanic of exploration and problem solving which made it the capstone of the genre). The improvement to an experience by pulling the puzzle out onto several screens is that you can start and stop at any time, encounter it in the wrong order, and suffer (through the mechanism of travel, which equates to time out of your life you’ll never get back taken by the torture device in The Princess Bride), so that the eventual success will be so much sweeter by contrast (to the suffering). 

Though it’s not really a gain when most single-screen puzzle games make basic repetition the price of advancing.

In any case, SMB and Contra avoid this (derisive snort) more cerebral approach to challenges, and encourage the building and sustenance of momentum as you travel through a world. You aren’t meant to reach dead ends, reconsider your approach, and explore (in so doing, facing waves of ’solved’ enemies repeatedly while you try to find the combination that will, basically, release the next puzzle, and its assortment of waves to sort through). Contra’s waves are always in sequence, unless you dawdle, and then you may have to deal with odd combinations and repeats. As time passed, Mario embraced the multiscreen puzzle, but no Contra, with the exception of the Super Nintendo edition, which included top-down maps which were navigable on your own schedule has ever tried that approach, and as a consequence, Contra is undiluted.

Shadow Complex is Controid.

The first level of Shadow Complex doesn’t even take place in the complex. You are treated to the best possible tutorial level — the tutorial level which isn’t one, and taught the basic thing you will need to know as you continue playing: your gun shoots bullets. Armed with this knowledge, the fact that, yes, there are rockets, and a double jump, and so forth, are all ancillary.

What Metroid fails to bring to Zelda, in terms of shooting, is that the typical shooter has enemies which are more capable than the protagonist (or, in the cases where the protagonist is in a plane, the protagonist’s plane). You fly with your shitty pea-shooter over miles of super tanks and rocket launching super carriers, who are all, at the very least, able to fly faster than you, backwards, whenever they feel like it.

Castlevania maintains this pattern. While you can jump and whip, and, in later games, uppercut, air dive, mist form, and wolf yiff (with pet fairy), what you fight is generally, by design, faster, stronger, and, most importantly, whereas you are small and wiley, your enemies are typically lumbering and covered in damageable spots (which make them more aggressive and dangerous, usually). The Metroidvania crossover implies a kind of gameplay where the forgiving nature of the strong protagonist with weak, mindless enemies, only dangerous in groups, is moderated by the deliberate challenge of the shooter against a vulnerable player. This usually ends up diluting the shooter qualities of the experience a great deal. Survival becomes much less complicated, and any time you feel you aren’t matched to the situation, the odds are, there is a method involving more time which could make the situation easier.

Controid, being established henceforth as The Next Big Thing, emphasizes the notion of time in a shooter environment as being an enemy, rather than a resource to be relied upon. The faster you find a solution to a given situation, the better your success.

DotA plays with concepts of time an urgency in ways that can be interesting, but are usually frustrating. The time it takes your unit to reach the fight, and the time you spend in vulnerable locations on the map while trying not to die are in your favor, in the sense that you accumulate resources which you will need to become effective — whether they’re growth in levels, or growth in cash to spend on money which is like free levels in an impossible-to-measure ether of complexity which makes judging any given situation in the game extremly randomized. However, the time you’ve spent in a given endeavor is also time you need to maneuver to the correct location for purposes of teamwork, or to work with your units to overcome the enemy’s static defenses. Ultimately, time meant to be saved can become time wasted, and vice versa. The more wasted time has accumulated, the more likely you will be unable to contribute, either because your character is insufficiently powerful, out of position, or engaged in tactically necessary defense of your base and it’s outlying defenses. Even shopping for your upgrades can potentially waste time.

But, the sliding scale does fuse two factors: the inevitable quality of certainty underpinning a Metroid or Zelda experience, where, ultimately, you will find a solution, as a matter of persistence, which can become boring, is tempered by the immediate need to find your roundabout solution as quickly as possible (which, unless I wasn’t clear before, is a great way to end up taking even longer), and the impossible-quality of the shooter when the odds are stacked against you, as typically personified in the bullet maze. Specifically, if you are out of position, you can’t *ever* catch up past a certain point, because there is no longer any margin. DotA finds a way to make a strategy game both be as steamrollerable as a fighter, and as convolutedly endless as a Daisenryaku. Metroidvania, similarly, has occasions when the enemies are as pointless and nonthreatening as a bat, and as utterly impossible as a 40-turreted space dragon that breathes lasers, all within the same situation (depending on items and approach).

Shadow Complex forges new territory, in that it is usually better to be faster, but not impossible to take your time, and, as the game progresses, the ability to be slow for certain challenges, becomes, like DotA the trade-off on wasting the time you might need, later, to succeed. In addition, the weirdness of complexity, especially on Insane difficulty, makes the situations in the game a best guess which emphasizes instincts rather than rote memorization of enemy behavior. The enemies can move in 3D while you are stuck along the 2D plane of your path through the world. This becomes their equivalent of flying faster or being stronger. With the added bizarre addition of headshots, the game becomes a shooter with the love of Metroid, and purity of Contra, while somehow tapping into the uncertainty of a cruel universe full of unknowns in a way quite different from its pedigree.

That said, you eventually get so much equipment that the game becomes a low impact playground — and there is no ecosystem, so the unpredictable nature of the shooter is somewhat limited, while time pressure becomes less important, eventually being utterly meaningless, even on the hardest difficulty mode, where a single (un)lucky shot could kill you instantly.

–samuel kite
