Starcraft 2 (***1/2)

a review of
starcraft 2: wings of liberty
a videogame developed by blizzard entertainment
and published by blizzard entertainment
score: 1/2 (out of four)

Bottom line: Starcraft 2 is “making out with other people.”

1/2

We think there’s something stimulating about the prophylactic quality of an RTS best expressed in the fact that you can play Starcraft 2 most satisfyingly by staying the fuck out of the regular game altogether, and just playing custom maps designed by people who want to market their barrier to entry to you, over the barrier to entry of the leading competitor.

Let’s say for the sake of argument that you could go back to the Atari Spiderman game, where you shot a web, released, and climbed a building. Ad nauseum. We thought that was fun for about one building’s worth. If it had somehow produced additional ‘challenge’ by coming up with ways to make you fall, then that would defeat the purpose of being spiderman, who, it is well known, can always catch himself by shooting a web off frame  in the panel where he’s drawn. If you come forward however many years, and play Spider-man: Shattered Dimensions, you get a similar experience. You repeatedly project through space, and arrive at the ‘end’ of space at some point. You can choose to do it again, or you can not. It looks better, it feels better, there is tremendously talented voice work, now, and even cool moments as you iterate through projection, but it is, in some fundamental way, the same game.

Starcraft and Starcraft 2 are utterly different.

We like cooking. We like it because we get absolute authority over what we eat, we like it because it’s a goal oriented task with nonspecific objectives and many methods. We like it because it’s practical.

We like it because it impresses chicks.

If you’re going to cook something, the first thing that seems to happen is you have to change your expectations about what you want to eat. When a mystery packet of yellow powder is dumped into milk and spread on tuna and noodles, or a mystery packet of red powder is dumped into ground beef and onions, or a mystery packet of brown powder is mixed with butter and flour and baked, the experience of the process is intimately connected to having no idea what it really was. We’re free to experience the high notes without knowing what it should taste like and comparing the two pieces of information.

We had no idea that Starcraft was supposed to be good. Someone made that decision after we got there. We were in the midst of Dark Reign and Total Annihilation. An era where past excesses of poor control and micromanagement were being addressed. Units were becoming more intelligent. User accessible scripts implied the ability to instruct units to take care of business and manage their behavior at a pace appropriate to the ever-spiraling upward complexity of the genre. More units, more bases, more tech trees, more resources, more map related considerations. What the fuck was Blizzard doing? Didn’t they get that their Death Knight asshole with all his hotkeys and extra units (which you couldn’t select, because you already had 5 orcs in the selection group with the Death Knight)  was some kind of archaic throwback to Warcraft: Orcs and Humans, when nearly-singular conflicts between units with virtually no will of their own wasn’t some design choice, it was the natural synthesis of a slippery concept of the RPG and Dune II (or, if not that, then some other satisfying explanation (if there is a satisfying explanation (it sort of doesn’t matter, despite tone, there’s no judgment here))).

It certainly was no Lost Vikings (maybe a little judgment).

Someone at Blizzard seems to think that a single RTS unit can be exciting. They think, at the end of the day, if you get it right, you can make a game out of doing nothing but playing that one RTS unit. It’s called World of Warcraft, and they seem to be right. They also seem to think that cooperation between individuals is more satisfying than cooperation within a single individual, or even basic versatility.

They have a Manchurian candidate scheme designed to activate people in the community and spawn things like DotA and Tower Defense.

Tribes 2 had a community ‘engine’ in place around the game. We had one tribal ‘scrimmage’, in which we repeatedly ran a jetbike to the back of the enemy base, hopped in, took their flag, and then hopped back out before their turrets or mines, or whatever they thought they were doing could stop us. The pinnacle of play in that game was taking a Shrike (this was a space ship with lasers on it), parking underwater (a delicate operation), and then tooling around down-river, until you could burst up into the air, like all the coolest Saturday morning cartoons, to no specific purpose. Lack of awareness in a first person shooter is the constant enemy. There’s one action figure to play with, but you don’t get the sense of staging. If we care about the cool things we’re doing, the entire game becomes about chatting over skype, or vent (or teamspeak or xbox live) and trying to get confirmation back (guys! Did you see that thing I did with my spinfusor? Guys? Did you see how I bounced my blaster shot around the corner and killed that guy?). Halo 3′s largest contribution to the genre of first person shooters was the ability to share the sentiment ‘dude did you see when I shot that guy?!’ with the entire world. In slow motion.

As an element of a product at a particular threshold of popularity.

The ‘professional’ gaming circuit, as a community is not about community. You can see it in the bizarre corporate inroads made with projects like Fragdolls or whatever Thresh is up to these days (note, Frag dolls are people, corporations that create frag dolls are not–though they can both, now, contribute to political campaigns). Starcraft, the original, spawned more overlords in this fashion. For Blizzard as a company to embrace community to the point of actually allowing map makers to come stay at their house and eat their All Natural White Cheddar Cheetohs, the fact that Starcraft became an anti-friendship game, only embraceable by a (arguably irrational) society so deeply immersed in impersonal interaction and virtual escapism as a means to find privacy in a day and life which has none (We’re generalizing, and also possibly incorrect–it’s kids these days, etc (why, in my day, you would simply go to your desk as a Salaryman every day, work your way up the ladder, and pay your mistress to beat you before coming home with half a paycheck worth of filthy 10k yen bills and claiming you were mugged to your barren wife (actually that would be Japan, and Korea is probably completely different)))) was probably the inspiration for a number of internally circulated emails fronting a speechless joy at the unprecedented popular acclaim of their work that concealed a deeper, monolithic, sad emoticon ( =:::| )

We’re not sure what Blizzard thinks about the things they’ve done. But we’re guessing that when they heard that kids in Korea were staying at their computers until they died, they were probably upset. They don’t want to make games that kill people. We don’t think they want to make games that even cause a tingling sensation that doesn’t go away within 2-3 hours. They gave a dying kid a phoenix. If you follow the World of Warcraft phenomenon as a instrument of depraved human abuse, what you may be surprised to find is that, as time passes it becomes kinder, gentler, and more genuinely worthwhile.

They tried to make it that way from day one, really. A question mark over an npc is the first timid step into interactive design for people who had played Everquest for years, and developed gnarled claws and hooked shoulder blades from carrying the carcasses of its victims to a burn pile. They had this ‘rested xp’ thing which allegedly encouraged people to log off. It didn’t *really*. In reality, it encouraged people to take an extra 15 minutes on the tail end of an obsessive series of monkey-pull-the-lever tasks to park their character in designated rest areas, rather than simply collapse in bed and begin having showing-up-to-class-naked nightmares about how they were going to lie, cheat, or scam, since they didn’t do their homework, and still have 40 pages of reading for the assignment, which they didn’t do because they kept seeing peacebloom.

Blizzard tried again, later, sometime after their first WoW pay-expansion. Making ‘money’ in their virtual economy was an unwelcome chore. It was too much like a full-time job for those who had the time to devote to it. So, instead, everyone would have access to easy daily quests that provided useful monetary gain and couldn’t be obsessively repeated by a minority of people with free time–thereby allowing casual players a way to even the score. Unfortunately in the zeal to provide variety,  a good 12+ hours of questing was added to the possible to-do list, every day, and for many with the affliction of outright humanity, if it was possible, it was required.

These gentlemen, though, they are not happy with their entertainment equivalent of a Bikini Atoll legacy. They want people to be happy. Persevering, years after the fact, they are slowly working with their intractable herd of cats to develop something very similar to a video game, that is fun, and could be played with friends. In another 3 years, they may get there.

It is a special kind of challenge to develop for a gamer community. Many players do not understand why they like what they like. They may incorrectly assume that their sense of accomplishment stems from hours and hours of mind-numbing tedium, or a spectacular string of luck resulting in unusual advantages (or both). If you were to give people access to things that these people enjoy, they might perceive it as a limited resource of pride that diminishes with each person who shares in it. For these people, there is a finite amount of pain in the universe which, when shared, is diluted. Assuming these people are noble and not selfish is the easiest way we know to cope with their existence, which you kind of have to, because you play with these people every day. It’s the internet, out there (picture us vaguely waving a gnarled root in a gesture that seems directed toward your modem).

Blizzard recognizes the fundamental truth about the RTS, that no other game type really comes as close to playing with the toys of childhood as it does. Furthermore, that the intrinsic quality of the RTS, to allow things to happen without direct intervention, and to demand that direct intervention, makes it the ideal lubricant between gamers. RTSes soothe the rough edges of synchronicity between people that a fighting game allows to hang out, and a coop game typically ignores, utterly.

First person shooters. The RTS unit experience. Single player. Humane treatment of those terrorized by entertainment. When you assemble these concepts you see a string of continuity between Blizzard’s projects, and, in a way, between Nintendo’s.

Blizzard and Nintendo, as amorphous entities act in opposition on the same goal. We do not mean they are fighting a war where one side will win, and the other will lose, we mean they are both trying to scale a wall toward a future of gaming, and they happen to be scaling different sides of that wall. They’re opposed like your thumb is opposed to the fingers of your hand.

If you dial in Nintendo’s quality in game design, you first end up at a single person (Shigeru Miyamoto, who’s whims are the creative seed lacked by the cybernetic brain of his corporate masters, and who’s delicious bodily fluids are the basis of every decent teriyaki sauce recipe (when not busy creating games, he is typically found in a traditional bath of chicken broth and what is probably molasses, soy sauce, and bonobo milk (his potency is capable of flavoring billions of gallons))), and you see a commonality between projects. They are typically 3rd person (AmeriMetroid will be addressed in a subsequent paragraph). They are typically characterized, but blandly. Link, for example, is similar to the flavor ‘Cherry Garcia‘. No one can mistake the cherries and chocolate, master sword, and green hat that make him what’s for dessert, generation after generation (of hardware). On the other hand, you need not know that he is an autobiographical entity, or an eternal child meant to discover the magic of forests and back yards forever and ever (with his kleptomaniacal backpack). What Link does is Adventure. To properly grasp Link’s place in his universe, you must spend most of your time in 3rd person. Placing ourselves inside something as an act of empathy or sympathy is easy. First person is unnecessary.

This is where Id and the Texan First person shooter production facilities seem to be in opposition to what Blizzard and Nintendo espouse. If you could nail down a single emerging feature of the first person shooter genre, as it grows into more popular forms, it is to be in 3rd person as often as possible. Whenever you can possibly get in a vehicle, for instance, or see yourself via a remote camera or the view of your opponent, it seems to take that opportunity. We have graduated from Halo, where, at every possible opportunity you attempted to see yourself fly or drive, to gears of war, where you drive your body, and only bother with first person for that visceral, and possibly disturbing experience of ‘Boom (comma) headshot’.

Which is Pokemon Snap with bullets.

The argument put forward by Idians in the past (and any offshoot of their mystique) is that nothing can be as immersive as the first person shooter. Arguably this is not the case. For one thing, you have no idea of a sense of self. When removing context (and no, a pair of wrists and hands holding the rear end of a shotgun that bobs and weaves like a combination between a river snake and a floppy dick does not offer much context), what you are left with is a living room filled with yourself, tv, and controller (or desk with monitor, mouse, and keyboard). The FPS perspective is alienating, not personable. For someone who approaches a work of art with the intention to be subjected to an experience, this perspective is effective. The fact that it is most often used to dump a Heavy Metal album cover into your face isn’t a limitation of the basic premise. Horror is typically and obviously about constrained perspective (Bioshock). But if your objective is to permit someone to play, in precisely the ways that playing and gaming differ from real life, then the focus has to be on permitting all those unreal things which life does not allow, to occur.

We had a longer version of this thought, but decided it was boring. Basically, 3rd person lets you see a thing which is not you–it’s a suitable superego chassis which is also an utter blank slate. It lets you experience enough of the environment that you can understand it and, more importantly, your place in it (with clarity that it suitably superhuman). Finally, these interactions try to culminate in a very special quality–the notion of added value.

Nintendo and Blizzard games tend to have infinity as a playable character or adversary.

In this era of Achievements, there is a tendency to treat completionism like a valid goal (when it is not). Completionism implies a lack of choice, even as it seems to be a byproduct of attempts to create choice. We think withholding outcomes from players is good. Some outcomes should be strenuously withheld for all time. The outcomes where everything worth doing is done and everything interesting has been seen shouldn’t be waiting like a pot of gold at the end of an FMV rainbow. In some cases (Final Fantasy: everything but tactics), games become about nothing except the attempt to end something enjoyable over and over again. Like a scenario in purgatory for Timmy who awakes to be faced with a gun and one bullet, and Yeller tied up behind the barn. You come to crave the moment of shooting that beloved pet (and best friend) because it’s the only point where you really get to have some (non) agency.

Some woman killed her baby because it interrupted her while playing Farmville. The obvious joke is probably something to do with games making you violent, but we say the reality is that Farmville is precisely the sort of game that trains a serial killer. It is a game in which the entire process is to shoot our rabies-infested dog over and over again. Until we have no idea why we bother. With the special additional ingredient that other people’s participation (or lack thereof) can frustrate our progress. In terms of a broad cultural activity, farmville, much like industrial farming, itself, represents the devastation of diverse environments and the destruction of nuclear families tied to their land symbiotically–engaging it with their personal interests and changing with it even as they renovate it. It is the inevitable consequence of unlimited time and money and the suggestion of activity implied by a ho.

Speaking of hos (this segue is all natural), the alternative to the inevitable destruction of an environment is the interplay of ‘players’ in the ‘game’ as individual actors which are not mutually destroyable. We’re not saying you might not set someone on fire in DotA or sap someone’s sentry in Team Fortress 2 (and be subsequently beaten to death by their righteous and vengeful pipewrench (spy playing bastard)), what we’re saying is that you cannot eliminate their participation for the duration of the event. Truly sustainable gaming is a matter of making out with other people, in a gaming sense (making out, perhaps, is the ultimate play behavior as it is a dry (or, if done right, fairly moist) run on the most important biological behavior (sneaking away before someone wants breakfast)).

When reviewing the mutual recrimination or extreme emotion that is the rule of competitive and cooperative play in sustainable playspaces, nothing is clearer than the notion of sexual compatibility. Why didn’t you run with the flag better (whatever that means), why couldn’t his timing have been more in tune with yours on that whole nuclear missile thing. The inability to properly express why things go wrong in team play is a matter of its intensely sexual nature. Every player is trying to produce in their experience moments of new life in the form of the stories told about victory, and when that life is stifled by incompatibility, there can be nothing more intolerable. Sure, you’re a nice guy, you have a great Kill:Death ratio. Your ranking is exactly the kind of thing we look for in match-making situations; we even appreciate your attentiveness to in-game chat and flexible schedule for clan practice matches. But we’re sorry, we just don’t think this is going to work out, in competition.

Blizzard’s core app is matchmaking service. In the realm of head to head, they want to make sure that nobody ever suffers through bad head (or head they don’t want (supposedly this is a real thing)) ever again (to head). In this case, head (to head) is the process of two players interacting. The RTS, as a venue, at first, seems to be a stilted control scheme lacking the immediacy and tactile feedback of any more legitimate interface (it is, after all, just a set of tools to manipulate objects in play (Diablo is as much a member of the RTS family as Starcraft or World of Warcraft, in terms of the controls used to participate, and the style of feedback)), but, whereas the original purpose of the RTS was a decidedly asexual extension of table-top turn-based wargaming (though, we suppose, the reality is that the direct and intense interaction of any chess-like derivative wargame between two men has an element of smoldering lust to it (the participants are probably doing us all a favor by keeping it strictly a matter of miniatures in their basement)) into a real time realm as a form of play-enhancement (you’ll never out flank someone as hard as you will in real time (‘My knees were shaking, and [...] I had welts’ -IGN)), it has become intensely personal.

Blizzard believes that to have a good time, whether you are working together, or against someone else (together), it is most important that you be able to fail. They might not like that thing you just tried. A typical fighting game is about interruption and momentum. Arguably it is an entire relationship. Ryu and Cammie are not just trying to determine who will win, they’re trying to decide how they can even cohabitate on the same screen. Someone needs an empty health bar, true, but more than that, someone needs to be convinced that the person who’s ‘got next’ deserves to take their place. The RTS isn’t about that ending. Rather than arguing that a character sucks because they do not conform to your expectations for play, an entire race (!) in an RTS are byzantine and difficult to keep track of, for the explicit reason that, if the purpose is a form of harmony that causes an ejaculation (e.g. ‘woo!’), they have to make you work for it, or it won’t have value.

It is frequently possible for a decisive battle to take place while armies are on opposite sides of the map, destroying opposing bases.

In this scheme, it becomes, then a very difficult proposition for Blizzard to design a system where you can fail, should fail, and are constantly challenged to avoid failure (by lifting a leg, rotating a hip, or nibbling on an earlobe at the right moment), yet provides all the tools necessary to make sure that when you fail, it is because of the people who are playing, and not the game itself.

Blizzard is engineering joyful user error.

We’re not very good at Starcraft 2.

Canabalt’s danger is in that you can never, ever stop for a moment, and a single error will kill you. But the essence of Spider-Man 2 presents no such obstacle (or at least none which is at all enjoyable to deal with).

    In this sense, SM2 is less a “Game” than a “Spider-Man Simulator.” This can be fun, but the emptiness of a complete lack of competent challenge to your Spider-Mannitude is what keeps it from being anything more than an idle distraction. A magnificent screensaver to what should be a much more robust game.
