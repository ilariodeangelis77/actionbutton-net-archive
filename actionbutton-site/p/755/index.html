Crackdown 2: Project Sunburst

30Nov2011

a review of Crackdown 2: Project Sunburst
a videogame developed by Microsoft Game Studios
and published by Microsoft Game Studios
for WP7
text by Samuel Kite

2 stars

Bottom line: Crackdown 2: Project Sunburst is “making a fort in your back yard.”

This was over before it started.

Crackdown 2 is a game in the vein of Prototype (**1/2 – ABDN), most recent spidermen, Infamous, Hulk, Other Hulk game who’s name I forget at the moment, Grand Theft Auto if you only play the part with the tank and military helicopters, and of course, Superman. Crackdown 2: Project Sunburst was a completely unrelated app that piggy backed on bing maps, and employed a magic-eight-ball style copywriter (apparently hell bent on giving the impression he’d like to break into punch-up for Disney films).

Everyone knows about cards. Cards are a robust system for gaming because they combine resources, randomness, and timing in a single gamepiece. Protocardian game pieces came either from the realm of tracking, or stamina management. The picture of how these activities broke down resulted in darts (scoring and resources without timing), chess (timing and resources without scoring), and any form of gambling (scoring and timing without resources–though, depending on the type of poker, that gets somewhat fuzzy (especially when, despite the Fed’s super-cool setup that gives the banks first dibs on imaginary money that the rest of us end up paying for in inflation, the average person still considers money to be real)). Dancing was probably also a relevant form of game play, but as it contained no pieces and was simply ‘fun’, cannot and should not be discussed by game design or game designers–as it is not sufficiently grave, and cannot be represented in terms of closets full of monsters, balanced, or rhetorically worked into some kind of metaconversation about mechanics. In fact, since dancing cannot find traction in a spreadsheet of any kind, unless it is butchered by a professional and sold off piecemeal as ‘moves’ or ‘combos’, it’s probably not even an artform, or the serious kind of activity that adults get up to when they are doggedly pursuing spiritual and intellectual growth, as all adults do, continuously, during every spare moment they have. Stop reading this frivolous crap, and pick up something by Kissinger to read while you powerwalk around the park with your rottweiler mix ‘rescue’.

In some sense there is a masculine/feminine dynamic within society that governs the way we develop play. If you look at apes, the propensity for male chimps to go hunting is related to female chimps ovulating. Whereas if you look at the behavior of lions and hyenas, the female lions are often organizing into pseudo militaristic groups operating without males who are called in to get into pissing contests with hyena matriarchs (who are adapted to positions of authority to the point of having fake penises) by the natural gravitation that all males have toward females (in mammalian circles, anyway). That’s from a national geographic video, by the way, which is was on netflix for a while, and still might be (‘lions vs hyenas’). In any case, if you watch closely, the struggle for territory is being driven by females in those two species, and the female lion essentially sets up the hyena for the male by tripping it. In these different species, there is a sexual division of labor on some level. There are varying degrees of cooperation between females in different populations (high in gorillas, low in hyenas (and to be clear, cooperation in this case boils down to the practice of gorilla females sharing the silverback as compared to matriarchal hyena society where fratricide (between daughters) seems fairly common (and matriarchs compete to establish hierarchy and protect their young from each other))) and social participation by males (high in gorillas to the point of caring for weaned orphan gorillas, and low in lions who seem to be out hunting with their bro for the vast majority of their lives). Some built in urge for dominance seem to be key to the development of these behaviors (almost hysterically obvious in the case of the female hyenas’ fake penises). In humans, much of the behavior is easily identifiable (men tend to be dominant, aggressive, and fairly cooperative and females are not focused on monogamy with the alpha male, particularly–similar to chimps, which is sort of seductive to out of control analogizing, since they’re our close relatives (but probably has nothing to do with video games)). The kicker when trying to identify where human behavior falls in the spectrum of mammalian social groups, is that we are highly adaptable in nearly every respect–vegetarian or carnivore, pacifist or ultraviolent, cooperative or independent. Arguably our unusual adaptability is the only meaningful advance over homo erectus, who had most of our other attributes (including fire, the very first video game (you have run out of fuel: Insert log to continue)). Play, as a fundamental part of our capacity to learn, which is at the core of our ability to adapt, then, when codified by frequent need and use into widely adopted games is a reflection of our environment to the extent that it is the learning behavior turning upon itself; to learn how to learn how to do (something (have fun)). In an ideal world, I would now offer a genuinely perceptive idea of what I consider to be masculine and feminine play, and everyone would be equally floored that I was so even handedly insightful, and also bored as hell because of the long list of caveats that would carefully state and restate, every few words, the fact that humans are adaptable, and there is no one-size-fits-all picture of gender roles or behaviors. Instead, I’m going to regurgitate the masculine point of view based on how I was taught it (ad hoc, through trial and error), which includes a kind of cliff notes of the feminine point of view–so that we, as males, can make sure we’re not acting like girls (and therefore fail the tests of life in a comprehensive, unforgivable way, according to the imaginary all father figure who’s trying to educate us as men (or started trying to educate us after agriculture started, and economic value was assigned to everything we do)). Unstructured nondominant artistic activities seem to have a feminine motivation, by which I mean, that’s the sort of thing a chick would like, bro, and maybe you should wear dresses if you think it’s cool. The contempt in male circles for certain activities (seduction, dancing, emotional expression) and female circles for others (physical dominance, sexual exploits, tests of endurance (usually of pain)) is a reflection of what gender role remains in the soup of adaptation, and is most interesting, perhaps, when it is suspended, particularly in games in which men and women have traditionally participated together. Card games, in particular, through an extremely broad set of game rules, are the playpen in which men and women have cooperated and competed in order to adapt to civilization.

Cards can be molded into a variety of game goals, and if you ignore the people playing, at the very least the dynamics of playing cards are usually affected by scoring. In gambling structures, like texas hold em, the scoring is about an iterative process, which, over time, creates a tug of war. Depending on the local variables stored for a given round of cards (whether the deck is shuffled between hands or whether the betting is limited (by pool, arbitrarily, or carefully measured exposure of supple breasts)), the state of the tug of war between participants in the hand can completely reverse, or merely move by set increments until someone decides or is forced to quit. In Bridge, the process of gambling and the overall betting is integrated into the playing pieces, and metagaming takes place involving bets placed on the prediction of scoring of the accumulated rounds, each of which is, itself, determined by a score. The basic interaction (playing a card (equivalent to a die roll, with a die that loses sides when it lands face up)) becomes terrain or a board in the abstract. In a derivative such as Hearts, opportunities such as ‘shooting the moon’ might exist to escape a tug of war state that would otherwise end in the inevitable trapping of a player.

In any case, all modern card game elaborations involve using cards to create identities (like creatures or characters), where their capabilities are either more specific or more generic, or don’t follow suit (or all 3).

Cards are a product of civilization, and, as hierarchy has been a plague on civilization, it is a plague on game design. Even at a superficial level, Monarchy is endemic to suits, the king is at the top. The Ace holds different values (either as highest or lowest) possibly as a religious message from the Bible; the first shall come last and the last shall come first. The European suits have several cultural sources. In one version, the Clubs are actually leaves or plants. In another Spades are swords, and Hearts are cups. The basic hierarchy told in the card suits is that soldiers are at the top, then wealth or royalty (and commercial art, in the sense that commercial art was a display of wealth for a private individual until the modern concept of a corporation made an individual impersonal), then the church (or arguably most other artists, since religion was the chief patron of the arts for long stretches of time), and finally, Farmers, at the bottom. The setting of the card decks reflects a desire for hierarchy that is either a practical understanding of reality, or else propaganda. That said, if you play an RTS, which takes creature cards and monarchy as fundamental underpinnings of its structure–a small monster (zergling) is a deuce and a large devastating spaceship (protoss carrier) is a jack–then the revelation that RTSes are almost all about resource management (as opposed to resources production), time being half resource and half scoring mechanism, seems to back up the idea that units are the most powerful lever, and winning the resource management race, in the course of the overall structure, is meaningless if it never made the units it takes to win (and won with those units). Dancing with your units can completely change their relative effectiveness, but because of its mysterious nature, even if it means you would win the round, like shooting the moon, it is a wild and crazy idea that contravenes all the applied knowledge about cards (that a Heart is a liability (in Hearts (but cannot be counted out))). The masculine perspective dominating this space is looking to create soldiers and wealth and to sacrifice your soldiers at the right moment before they succumb to a stripper (with her vile dancing). Better to die than have fun.

Clearly the tacit assumption of all card games is that women’s bodies are dirty.

There has been, for a couple years, a persistent development in the flash game community of the tug of war genre, which, having been transferred to Starcraft 2 will now experience a Tower Defense-like evolution into genre parity. The principle of Tug is to play a card game in which synchronous or asynchronous waves of cards are sent down predefined paths without direct intervention (or else strictly gated direct intervention meant to happen at specific intervals, which seems to do nothing but add the element of screwing up as a concession to masculine interests), while a metagaming system of ‘betting’ (on the opponents choice of cards, mostly) controls the resource stream which allows someone to play more or better cards (in some cases, the resource stream is independent of the performance of cards, which, judging by the most popular Starcraft 2 custom maps is preferable to the snowballing resource situation). Built in to the units are combinations like ‘full house’ of 3 strong cards (hydralisks) and 2 tough cards (roaches), which have a certain practical effect on the map that isn’t obvious in the evaluation of a strong card (single hydralisk (who is lonely, but, due to developmental derangement, does not want a hug from your firebat–and can only express its inner turmoil over this fact with toxin filled darts and ineffectual flailing with its scythelike dinosaur arms)). But, in all representations of these games, there is a dancing component involving the use of the interface (usually double-blind and consisting of an avatar as the point of contact with the deck of cards). It’s what spawns the cards sent into battle–and the placement of deck objects can have an organizational, communication, or even card-efficiency related effect (also like dancing). However, since dancing is frivolous, couldn’t possibly be considered art, and most likely the province of the devil, who causes men to sin with his/her overt sexuality, must be covered in a black tarp, and never discussed in game design while righteous men still take their place at podiums.

Sunburst’s structure is ultimately one of tower defense, not tug, and, since it presents no definitive ‘challenge’ per se, being a mobile game that takes a Farmville like approach to building lasers that cultivate city streets free and clear of mutants (a subtly autocratic and disquieting premise (almost more upsetting than Crackdown 2‘s presentation of the Agency as a monolithic big brother organization out of control)), with the occasional barn raisin’ of a new rocket launcher, and a very odd, almost parental spur to move around your local area in order set up new ‘bases’ that help you find digital geocaches of imaginary resources–it has no pressures, and, really, requires no useful inputs, though it does permit the precise placement of turrets and structures as they relate to roads on the map, where the mutants you must purge accumulate.

This indefinable aspect of choice is exactly the kind of thing that doesn’t go on a spreadsheet, so I won’t talk about it.

The Bible seems to argue the God created the universe at roughly the time domestic agriculture became a major food source. Not coincidentally, at this time, people immediately got shorter, shorter lived, lost more teeth, and seemed to sprout a variety of social structures and practices, which include usury, formalized slavery (as distinct from tribal adoption and abuse), monarchy, hereditary wealth, various types of career soldier or warrior, and military practices involving destruction of population rather than displacement. For instance, Assyrians were known to have slaughtered all the men in a city, and enslaved the women and children. There’s no point contending that such a thing couldn’t happen in a hunter gatherer society, just that, if it did, it was on a smaller scale, more personal, and a decision carried out ad hoc, if at all, since hunter gatherer resources were as mobile as people (the resources were generally animals). There’s plenty of reasons to be explored, in terms of nutritional deficiencies in animal fats, and proteins, as well as the effect insulin resistance has on the population which was fed primarily by the grain-based food supply full of phytic acid and gluten, but what is more relevant to a spreadsheet is the notion of social structure and the power plays within that structure.

In personal relationships, rules, in many cases, are of a secondary consequence (or nearly non-existent). If we were to play cards with our dearest darling, and determine that, one way or another, we will win together–if either of us wins, then we have, effectively, both won, and can happily sacrifice our game position for the completely unspreadsheetlike goal of getting laid in the metagame. The game rules are subverted, in the same way altruism as a survival trait is subverting the organism metagame. Reasonably sophisticated games like Hearts take into account alliances and wrap it up in the fundamental structure of the game to attempt to make it a recognized part of play. However, it can essentially be ‘outdone’ by taking on a second partner in the metagame, and working together to sacrifice yourself for the interests of an in-game structured pair–for instance if they’re hot sisters who’ve gone through 1 and a half bottles of the merlot you brought over, and your buddy is kind of oblivious to what’s going on, and keeps trying to beat them at cards for some reason.

But in the context of a larger anonymous society that is possible with agriculturally supported urbanity, or, in the grim darkness of the far future, the internet, these metagame alliances are, themselves, subject to random failure, and an increasing mutual strangeness which makes them harder to establish and maintain. For example, in a bygone era when there were not ~7 billion people on earth, you might learn to adapt and appreciate people of all (local) attitudes and urges more readily, because you are forced to deal with them and your options were stoic isolation in the mountains (where the bears are), or (the now filthy dirty term) ‘settling’ (for someone with a decent dowry, a clean well, a good mule–or whatever else they have for sale). The legend of true love was based around the relatively few cases in which two people in a relatively sparse community were of the right age for each other, and shared an intense chemistry. No one rationally expected that to share hobbies and interests. Sharing hobbies was the sort of thing one might do when bored and all your offspring had succumbed to mysterious illnesses in the twilight of life. Sewing circles were hospice care. For the at all vigorous, there was the farm, or the family business which was not a farm, or else the process of hunting, if we go even further back in time. Loyalty was prized as a matter of duty to particular responsibilities, not as a thought-control device.

However, in the post agricultural era, all sins are mortal, even when venial, or perhaps especially then, and as a result, we are now largely driven to avoid genuine growth, and instead discard anything which does not fit as quickly as possible on the assumption it will actually cause us to physically shrink.

As a gameplay structure, discards are a vital civilized concept. In games such as Hearts, there are no true discards. All cards are used at all times and a discard is best represented by a play where the outcome does not affect metagame success.

Gaming is play behavior, and play behavior is a learning tool. In the hunter gatherer or herder environment, all play behavior revolves around competition and cooperation. Hierarchy is achieved through personal relationships. In the post agricultural society, concentration of population leads to strangeness, and the deliberate and labor intense nature of farming leads to play behavior revolving around rules. Hierarchy controls personal relationships, since some external authority governs the availability of people to participate in a relationship with each other. Play behavior, then, focuses on the manipulation of authority rather than performance in the game, itself (bluffing).

One of the most popular hunter gatherer games was some form of gambling. The competition is direct and personal, and typically results in temporary assets (perishable) being transferred between parties. Inevitably, the choice to participate and the determination to quit are a matter of deciding how viable other concerns are, such as how easy it is to get food, or whether some kind of direct trade or favor could be employed instead. Ultimately, gambling for people who have no substantial concept of transferable wealth (even up to herders, who typically have a raiding culture by which wealth in the form of animals changes hands, frequently without substantial bloodshed) is not perilous, and so the act of gambling is purely an element of the game. Nomads tending a herd cannot account for or ‘lock up’ every animal in their possession at all times, and, thus, stealing them back and forth is easier than trying to preempt stealing altogether. Sedentary agricultural civilizations typically had difficulty bribing warlords for this reason–the act of raiding was as much a sport as it was genuine survival motivated attack (and, more generally, ‘civilized’ farmers had little that herding cultures would want–since they didn’t even have compatible economic perspectives).

In chess, sedentary agricultural civilization’s form of gambling involved bluffing, and sports that developed to amuse the upper class (like jousting, fencing, wrestling, etc), who were, themselves, playing a social metagame that revolved around the outcome and sponsorship of sports (and rituals for the sports). The ‘hands’ for this competition were the control and falsification of information (about the competition) and abuse of personal relationships (both of competitors and between sponsors–a modern example that illustrates this well is in the movie Moneyball). In chess, the personal relationship between opponents has little room for genuine guile; knowledge of your opponent’s confusions and focus is the only valid means to winning–otherwise, chess is merely a massively complex version of tic tac toe–one side should inevitably win (most likely the one that goes first) given perfect knowledge of all evolutions of the board. However, since such knowledge is impossible (probably), the only recourse left is to find ways to evaluate an opponent, mislead them, and maneuver the game into an area of outcomes recognized by yourself, and not the other person. In some cases, maneuvering into blind territory is useful provided you know it is blind territory for both (discarding what certainty you do have for the chance at more favorable outcomes or unsettling you opponent). People with minor knowledge of chess often make moves which are merely unexpected to try to discover likely paths to success while their opponent is reevaluating, rather than working through move and counter moves that are obvious, even if seemingly necessary (for example, not protecting a powerful piece that is threatened, in favor of moving their own piece into a threatening position–even if it is an unequal potential trade). While chess is directly competitive and has a clear winner and loser in most cases, the intimacy of the anticipation of another person for an extended period of time is similar to dancing, except that, in dancing, unsettling your opponent and knocking them off balance is unwelcome.

The notion of bluff is occasionally explored, now, in game mechanics. R.U.S.E. is probably the clearest example of a bluff based game, its first and greatest bluff is probably the idea that it has anything whatsoever to do with WWII–including the use of period correct icons to communicate the purpose and strength of units (they do not, particularly). But this is not the extent of bluffing in game design. The use of ‘cancel’ (unironically a discard) in fighting games allows, both, for valid repurposing of the time resource in that style of game, and the loss of the time wasted in initiation before the cancel. Similar to losing a card from a hand for the later accumulation of either a won hand, a preferred card, or another opportunity at the chance for some better position. In cards, discards carry an additional penalty of communication, in that they signal a desire for increased mobility or change. Manipulation in this manner is most similar to lying. They can communicate weakness as a ploy to subsequently communicate bluffed strength. In a fighting game structure, cancels are typically a reaction to something specific the opponent did (like move out of range), and communicate strength automatically in the broader sense of having increased opportunity for choice–but can be used to bluff weakness by inappropriate use, as a ploy to make your opponent aggressively overextend themselves.

Wasting assets as a form of metagame communication to mislead an opponent is at the core of industrial and political waste within the post agricultural society. However, in the form of art, wasting assets (in a crude sense of using resources, time, and energy to produce things which are inedible (as well as instrumentally inedible (or presented as allegedly useful as in the case of marketing or entertainment))) as a manipulation is entirely positive (or at least not automatically negative). In this case, the manipulation on an interpersonal level is occasionally pedantic (fur is murder!), but can merely be the generic production of beauty to inspire, or the grist for thought which may inspire more art. It is organically the use of waste to achieve utility and purpose, as in the case that the waste byproducts of genetics becomes viruses and bacteria, which can, eventually, become symbiotic with larger organisms, despite independence, and in the midst of this a robust diversity is, itself, a mechanism of enriched survival. Which is a positive contribution of agriculture despite the simultaneous invention of a niche for parasites in the form of management.

Making decisions about who’s the host organism, and who’s benefiting from the existence of a foreign entity is, itself, an additional metagame which occurs in a competition between rulesets to determine which form of game is more important, and, in pursuing that game, which personal relationships will be tapped. If a government can convince its people that its existence guarantees theirs, in the same way that a farmer’s continuing existence guarantees that there will always be chickens to slaughter and eat, then the people can become convinced that they are the parasites, and the government is the organism they inhabit (instead of the other way around).

If you have friends who like fighting games, and you want to hang out with those friends, then you usually have to learn how to play fighting games a little bit. The addition of social gaming in the arcade setting, the invention of 3rd party arcade sticks for that experience on console, and recently, internet matchmaking in games like Smash Brothers, can cause a fracturing of the existing social structures. Whereas a group might choose, in the past, to play games serially, moving together on to the next great thing, the mobility and privatization of social gaming experiences draws people toward individual quirks and loyalties to franchises or products which fit their personal idiom. As a result, the original social groups are divided, and a great measure of control and wealth is gained by the entities that perform the fracturing. They are engaged in a discard of playerbase resources with the interest in creating ‘new markets’ from positions of assumed weakness or strength (depending on who they’re trying to snare).

Ultimately, looking at Project Sunburst, there’s not much going on. It is extremely passive and has a mean interaction time of something like 10 minutes (and don’t think just because I said mean interaction time that I give a shit or measured it). During that time, you may find input unnecessary to the point that your phone falls asleep. At other times, it can feel like a chore to be completed–opening the program and burning up the tiny battery life of your new toy to go through a dozen imaginary bases and upgrade rocket launchers or deploy sandbags.

But there is a charm when you go to a friend’s house, sit down, pop the phone out, and announced that they’ve got a quote ‘serious mutant issue in the neighborhood’ and set up a new base. It’s a moment straight out of the honest innocence (a cannibalistic, psychotically uneven and unpredictable (yet ultimately kinder) innocence) of early childhood, where a statement like ‘I put a laser turret in the trees across the street to protect your house from monsters’ is just the kind of stand up favor anyone might do for a bro. I guess if you’re a girl, you might cast a spell to protect your castle from goblins? I suppose it’s possible girls are discouraged from playing protector.

Damn Patriarchy.

In any case, what you come up with is an urge to show a friend this thing, and have them participate. You build a fort on their house, and they build one on yours. In Sunburst is a provision to deploy your friends as assets to defend an area for you. This is usually most charming if they are defending their own house–the process of which you are overseeing by building more lasers, and ordering those lasers to destroy things, together.

You end up spending snatches of time during the day with your friend, vicariously, by defending his house from monsters with his Xbox LIVE persona–using it like a doll. It is similar to talking to a dead spouse when you do those little things during the day that you might have cause to share a thought with them (Oh look at the newspaper, oil spill etc (jerks! (Those poor pelicans))), but they are not there (for instance, because they are in the ground). Since they didn’t have a speaking role in those moments of togetherness, you can simply speak to them, imagine they can hear, and be satisfied (if that works for you). In this way, Project Sunburst is a robust tool for maintaining faith in a personal relationship outside the more time consuming MMO, multiplayer online, or multiplayer couch gatherings. The ultimately hurdle to togetherness is that it requires time and another person. Sunburst minimizes both these requirements.

For the mere introductory price of 4$.

And an Xbox live subscription.

And a 2 year contract for a WM7 phone.

And the phone itself (200$ if I send in the rebate)

They need all this stuff too.
