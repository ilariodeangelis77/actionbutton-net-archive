Detective Comics Universe Online **1/2

a review of
detective comics universe online
a videogame developed by sony online entertainment
and published by sony online entertainment
text by Samuel Kite
score: 1/2 (out of four)

Bottom line: DC Universe Online is “a peculiar fusion of Capcomian surprise and Blizzardian feature completeness”

1/2

There are two prevailing attitudes toward competition. They emphasize foresight or talent. The lowest common denominator between them is counting on the mistakes of your opponent. But when possible, it is far more entertaining to allow successes to carry unusual weight for two reasons:

1) Iteration. An accumulation of failures which create the difference between an ideal performance and the actual competitive result can make for an inevitable loss long before that loss actually occurs, and the nature of the failures usually implies that any avenue you have for adjustment will be difficult to inject–you may have to wait for the majority of a round or match before reaching the critical stage where a mistake you made last iteration cost you the overall competition. Overwhelming critical success makes games *end* and allows them to start again–and are more actionable. You can counter a success, you cannot counter attrition. In this way, critical success can create a metagaming opportunity for rounds of complete games, making the rounds of the game itself, turns, and the turns within a resource of time with provisionally equal access for all competitors.

2) Fatigue. Losing due to carelessness is a side-effect of time and is not satisfying for the victor or the loser.

Three. 3 reasons.

3) Intangibles. If outrageous success can occur quickly, then it becomes far more interesting to work with the intangibles of the gameworld, such as placement, the visuals over which you have control, ‘surprise’, and communication. You see this in Defense of the Ancients, for instance, where going into range can often mean automatic death, unless you can fake someone out into wasting a cooldown or going too far out of position–or exposing themselves to be ambushed while you sacrifice yourself.

Four. Four reasons.

4) Emotional Aesthetics. Personal triumph is shareable through a team mindset only in a metagame *and* is uplifting for the people who experience it. Diffuse winning spread through a group of people who didn’t screw up together for a set amount of time is the methadone equivalent of Dramamine(sic) for the terminally anything-but-easy-listening-and-light-jazz-averse (PVE players).

In summary, I believe that aversion to quick kills and hopeless situations is a product of a kind of pervasive fear culture which coddles people to the point that any given stereotype of a carebear, My Little Pony loot mule, or frothing Vampire The Masquerade LARPing teencreature refuse to play pvp at all, because they would rather participate in the pious diligence of a raid, as if plowing a virtual field to reap a harvest of epic corn in keeping with the desires of The Lord, than experience the possibility of being at the short end of some stick, even if they themselves could offer the short end to others (turning the other mascara and cold cream caked cheek).

In fact the etymological cul de sac in which we’ve arrived that presupposes a division between pve and pvp is at the very fusible crux of the entire tissue (back foul beast, my breath smells of garlic toothpaste)–by which I mean a wafer thin delicate membrane immediately destroyed by the tears and snot of those that blow into it (honk).

In contrast to the Pixar-animation-demo quality of travel in the Everquest era, or the only slightly more exciting snail’s pace of soulless mathematical translation which carries a character through WoW’s invisible coordinate system, the basics of movement in DCUO are similar to Prototype or Spiderman, as measured by the joy of moving around. Merely achieving this would make it remarkable for an MMO, but the choice of quests and arrangement of the career mechanics (that is to say, the arc of your progression, the locking of mechanical advantage achieved at a given fulcrum of gameplay for the length of your lever, and the suggestion of who’s world you’re going to move, in the shape of quests that invite you to destroy digital lives (if you know what I mean (,baby))) makes it an experience which does not understand MMO politics: fear of confrontation, or hurt feelings. It is more like a playground for superheroes where genuine unfairness happens (like any given playground, where unfairness is the chief grounding physical and social law which achieves the ultimate victory of creating a person who plays a game of finding out how to have fun (taking personal responsibility for joy (possibly allowing you to provide joy to others (who comes first in your bed, hmm? (do unto others (The gentleman behind the Curly Mustache does his opponents the courtesy of finding their g spot (note, if you take this too seriously and are angered, then you’re missing the point of playing games and what fun is for))))))).

Simultaneously, it (the game) has a much more condensed mechanical spectrum of power differentials meaning that ultimately, the insanity of unfairness is also uncharacteristically fair. The quality of a tool, particularly a simple tool (lever; fulcrum) is that it is universally attainable. This quantum leap in fundamental execution in the MMO space is unprecedented. Utterly. It’s too good to be Japanese and too good to be American. What we are seeing is the first generation of pan pacific cooperation toward a gaming future. It’s like Capcom and Blizzard had a baby–a fusion of Capcom’s ‘you had no idea you wanted this, but here it is; this robot piloted by a baby can pick up a flamethrower and cook a turkey for extra health’ and Blizzard’s ‘in a swarthy concoction of competing desires that are often contradictory, we discovered the Matrix like quantum computation of the perfect path which satisfies each and every desire of anyone who could possibly enjoy our offering, whatsoever’ (by which I mean, the assumption that the ‘One’ quality which characterizes the problem of Free Will in the machine world is the efficiency of a quantum principle of computation which could, in the fiction of that universe, result in instantaneous and perfect decisions that cannot be matched by linear computation, which, when bent against an existing system, result in entropy (represented by the linearly iterative security initiative by a rogue process which ends up finally demanding all available resources in order to achieve equality with the destabilizing process-becoming, itself, a destabilizing process, and ultimately requiring a forced ending to the process of control which is inimical to the desires of the best-fit path, which, in this case, is probably a metaphor for frustrated gender identity as expressed in elaborate metaphor involving synthetic evolution (which seems appropriate since a sex change will involve some synthesis (Tautology: fiction is fiction))).

If the basic structure of social change in the modern era involves the mutual antagonism of different powerbases, then the fusion of previously separate entities is always a time for something exciting to happen. For example, church and state, or military and industry, or more recently, art and industry. Perhaps in the event cultural accumulation around a need for sharing the most interesting aspects of daily life with complete strangers halfway around the globe is ultimately revisiting a fusion of art, (as represented by its coopted form: the church (or mosque or whatever (organized spiritual fanfiction))) liberty, and the truly democratic underpinning of the state, the people, as they commingle in their shared narratives, they are actually participating in a sponsored uprising (By SOE, in desperation to shake loose the domination of spaces they formerly owned, outright).

In that light, the animosity between Superman and Lex Luthor has this subtext of the perspective of clergy as though jesus christ were a real physical force wrestling with secular bourgeoisie, as part of the merchant class, for power over the peasantry–a downtrodden mass being overwhelmed by robotic experiments, death rays, fluoride in the drinking water, statins prescribed to women and men with no history of heart disease, and hydrogenated vegetable oils. In this case, christ is so ultimately just that his class warfare is completely defensive–despite having crashed on earth in his escape pod to bring not peace, but a sword, he is still such a (rock hard, chiseled, and handsome) lamb, that the money changers in the temple assault him, or the innocent victims of heteronormative society (such as the homeless, minorities, mutants, women, children of broken homes, or spirits of dead gods haunting forgotten relics, who’s only crime is to have gone mad from near eternity in limbo (it’s adam and eve, not adam and Hrothgar, protectorate of the world crystal)), and thus seal their fate (which is to be cast into hell after he uses the momentum of his turning cheek to spin completely around, and round-house kick sinners into another of 52 core dimensions (for more explanation on this, see issues 510, 674, and 894 of Superman: the man of Steel, Wonderwoman, Green Lantern, Justice League, and The Amazing Spiderman, though not The Incredible Spiderman, or just Spiderman, as neither of those periodicals contain information about this particular nugget of comic bookery — Ed).

That overstatement of a spiritually dramatic position in a space of allegedly greater concerns where a society plays ‘games’ with finances such as inventing money, gambling with the ability of millions of people to own housing, afford healthcare, or, for that matter, the basic necessities of life, such as whether an african child will eat subsidized corn meal, or a more well-rounded meal of dirt and the occasional endangered elephant, is just one of the many subjects which DCUO does not cover explicitly.

It is a peculiar fusion of Capcomian surprise and Blizzardian feature completeness, that DCUO has quests such as ‘push a suicidal NPC off a building’ or ‘help someone steal a car and then double cross them after bribing a cop’, or, best of all, ‘go kill several low level players’. Obviously, these are villainous acts to be executed by villains, but the mere existence of that final imperative to do what you do anyway, but with our blessing and a cash bonus is really the final step of agreement between a worldwide oppressed mass of diaspora and the corporate harkonnen, willing to risk all inherited privilege for the chance to attain the top position. The gamer has been accused of murderous intent, social awkwardness, and an undermining of those nonspecific social values which are held so dear by a begged question. That construct, itself, is not driven by a notion of mass appeal, but an a priori declaration by moneyed interests. Our art and society is ushered toward fears and conflicts because they are good for business. Should I hate PS3s and beat a great bear-skin drum while shrieking at the top of my lungs for the 360? If I do, it is because that controversy might drive money into landfill like structures tended by monolithic entities who’s interest in art is as a taco shell to contain lettuce shredded and picked in the argentaria populi.

So here is this experimental thing birthed by a an awkward corporate breeding program, which has created a noble mule of exciting potential. I can’t say that DCUO will thrive. Like Armada, Phantasy Star Online, or Rocket Jockey, DCUO is a nearly mythical Tronlike experience almost too completely perfect for its time period to be real. If you’ve ever played an MMO before, and have any expectations from that experience, yet still feel any desire, whatsoever, to play MMOs, those expectations will be confounded.

Why, then, does this game merit 2.5 stars on the bedrock of absolute truth that is the subjective heuristic actionbutton.net rating system?

When you dive face first into a soldier from several miles up in X-Men Origins: Wolverine (*** – ABND), and turn it into a can of tomato paste by crushing it under an angry canadian missile of sharp metal sideburns, what happens next is a series of very precisely timed moments. Filleting a human being may be redundant, it may be excessive, but it will never be anything but a finely tuned moment of catch and release; a sportsman’s orgasm of success. When you introduce the looseness of the ‘massive’ experience, and its exposed mechanical innards (A number that floats), the collapse of that man’s trachea takes on a quality slightly akin to fumbling with a key as you try to insert it in your front door lock (or the more obvious fumbling that will happen next if you can make it to the couch without anyone passing out). When you slip out (‘oops, sorry baby’), it kills the mood a little bit, and brings you back to earth where, rather than a pornographic fantasy of a Marvel Vs Capcom union between finely tuned Japanese artistic mechanics, and the hydraulic pumping action of American diversity, competition, and narrative integrity in even the strangest of circumstances (on the one hand, yes, it’s Mega Man fighting a Sentinel–on the other hand, whereas the anime often takes the finest ingredients and makes a pile of mush, the broadway play takes base tripe and spices, and makes the WWF (before you turn your nose up at that, give it the benefit of the doubt that creating any kind of narrative from nearly nothing–including nurturing actors who’ve come from an environment which has no outlet for that talent–is a humanist work–even if it is, again, in the service of someone making money)).

There’s no going back, now. Ever since Metroid successfully executed the first person perspective with an American team working under Japanese creativity, the tentative exchange of phone numbers and promises to ‘call you this weekend’ between gaming traditions has been made. The French have reasserted themselves with a newfound polish in the form of Ubisoft’s various Mirror’s Edgeesque Prince of Persia iterations (coming a long way from the days of Dune, when the Spice Flowed from bizarrely compelling gameplay into a trough of no audience, to the current day and age when a whimsical adventure of an arabian prince fighting the shadows of entropy as he seeks to undo the sacrifice of a kingdom which gave him the gift of true love, is performed by the shirtless hollywoodite with a 5 o’clock shadow that says ‘hey baby, after I kick this sand wizard’s ass, I’m going to give you a pg13 kiss–so the audience can pump their fists and stomp their feet like Hessians trampling daffodils on their way to Moscow). If the big sister of gaming last generation has married Jacque, it’s now gratifying for the whole family to see the little brother of this generation start dating Tomoko, who, after all, is such a pleasant and smart girl–and so polite to her elders.

DCUO, is a landmark moment in democratic art finally overcoming the inertia of vested interests.

It is also still an MMO.

– Cosmic Muffet for Action Button dot Net
